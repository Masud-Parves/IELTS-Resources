## Part 1 — Idea Generation Flow {: .part}

<div class="flow">Family → Nuclear vs Joint Family → Caring for Elderly People → Generation Gap &amp; Time Together → Grandparents &amp; Grandchildren → Parenting: Discipline vs Nurturing → Working Parents &amp; Childcare → Children's Freedom &amp; Independence → Parental Pressure → Technology, TV &amp; Family Life → Gender Roles, Marriage &amp; Having Children → City or Countryside for Children → Family Values, Personality &amp; Success</div>

When you see **any** family or society question, run through this chain from left to right and ask: *"Does this question touch this area?"* Then **stop at the 2 links that fit the question best**. Those 2 links become your two body paragraphs.

**Memory trick — 3 generations × 3 forces of change**

Think of the **3 generations** in a family and the **3 forces** that are changing family life:

| | **Work** (both parents working, long hours, migration for jobs) | **Technology** (phones, TV, social media) | **Urbanisation** (moving to cities, small flats, nuclear families) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Grandparents** | children too busy to visit; elderly care problems | cannot use new technology; feel left out | left alone in villages; loneliness |
| **Parents** | stress, less family time, need childcare | work emails at home; screen time rules | smaller homes, no relatives to help |
| **Children** | less time with parents; more independence | screens replace play and talk | less outdoor space; less contact with relatives |

Find the generation(s) in the question, then ask which force explains the change. You will quickly have reasons, effects and examples.

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Example:** *"Grandchildren rarely spend quality time with their grandparents. What are the consequences?"*

Generations: **Grandparents + Children**. Forces: **Urbanisation** (families live in different cities) + **Technology** (children prefer screens).
Consequences → BP1: grandparents feel lonely and unwanted; BP2: children lose traditions, values and family history.

</div>

### 2A. The 5-Minute Plan

| Minute | What to do | What you write on the question paper |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | **Decode the question.** Underline the topic words and the instruction words. Decide which of your five essay guides it uses (Part 2G). | e.g. *elderly care / family or government → Discuss Both Views guide* |
| 1–3 | **Generations × forces grid.** Who is affected? Which force (work, technology, urbanisation)? | 4–6 one-line notes |

### 2E. How One Idea Becomes a Full Paragraph (PEEL)

**P**oint → **E**xplain (why? how?) → **E**xample → **L**ink / Result

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### বাংলায় চিন্তা (Bangla thinking)

**Point:** যৌথ পরিবারের সবচেয়ে বড় সুবিধা হলো পারস্পরিক সহায়তা।
**Explain:** কারণ একই বাড়িতে কয়েক প্রজন্ম থাকলে শিশুদের দেখাশোনা ও বয়স্কদের যত্ন ভাগাভাগি করে নেওয়া যায়।
**Example:** যেমন, বাবা-মা দুজনেই চাকরি করলে দাদা-দাদি স্কুল থেকে ফেরা শিশুদের দেখাশোনা করতে পারেন।
**Link/Result:** ফলে শিশুরা নিরাপদ থাকে এবং বয়স্করাও নিজেদের প্রয়োজনীয় মনে করেন।

</div>

**The same idea in English (one paragraph):**

> **Point:** The greatest advantage of a joint family is mutual support.
> **Explain:** When several generations live in the same house, they can share the care of children and elderly relatives.
> **Example:** For example, if both parents work, the grandparents can look after the children when they return from school.
> **Link:** As a result, the children are safe, and the elderly members feel useful and valued.

Count it: about **60 words** from one idea. Add a second explanation (for example, shared costs of rent and food) and you reach the **100–120 words** that one body paragraph needs.

### 2G. Essay-Type Paragraph Plans (your five essay guides)

| Essay type (guide) | Introduction | Body Paragraph 1 | Body Paragraph 2 | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Agree / Disagree** (*to what extent? / do you agree?*) | **35–50:** paraphrase + *I completely agree/disagree with this view because* Idea 1 and Idea 2. Never "partly". | **100–120:** Idea 1 — P E E X L | **100–120:** Idea 2 — P E X R L (*Although some people argue that …, [my side]*) | **35–55:** *In conclusion, I firmly believe that …* + both ideas in new words |
| **Discuss Both Views** (*some think X, others think Y*) | **35–45:** *While some people argue that [other view], others believe that [my view]. I completely support the latter view because …* | **90–120:** the view you do **not** support, no "I" — P E E X L | **90–120:** **your view** — P E X R L | **35–45:** *In conclusion, people hold different views on whether … While …, I firmly believe that …* |
| **Advantages – Disadvantages** | **40–50:** paraphrase + *Although* [side you do not support], [side you support] + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **30–40**, 2 sentences) | **120–135:** advantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **120–135:** disadvantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **35–45:** *While …* + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **25–35**) |
| **Cause/Problem + Solution** | **30–40:** paraphrase + *This is mainly because C1 and C2, but S1 and S2 can tackle this problem.* No opinion. | **90–120:** 2 causes or problems — P E X P E L | **90–120:** 2 solutions, each fixing one cause (WHO + WHAT + HOW) | **30–40:** causes + solutions in new words |
| **Double Question** (*two questions*) | **30–40:** paraphrase + *[Answer 1], and [answer 2].* | **90–120:** answer to Q1 — List block (P E X P E L) or Opinion block (P E X R L) | **90–120:** answer to Q2 — List or Opinion block | **30–40:** *In conclusion,* answer 1 + answer 2 in new words |

## Part 3 — Category-Wise Free Writing {: .part}

Each passage below is an **idea store**. Read it slowly, notice how each idea is explained and supported with an example, and pay attention to the **bold collocations**. After each passage, try to say 4–5 of its ideas aloud in your own words.

### Nuclear Family versus Joint Family

The structure of the family has changed significantly in many parts of the world. Traditionally, especially in South Asian countries such as Bangladesh, many people lived in **joint or extended families**, where grandparents, parents, children, uncles, aunts and cousins shared the same home. Today, more and more people live in **nuclear families**, consisting only of parents and their children. Both types of family have advantages and disadvantages.

The greatest strength of the joint family is **mutual support**. Family members share responsibilities, such as cooking, cleaning, childcare and caring for elderly relatives. When both parents work, grandparents can look after the children, which saves money on childcare and ensures that children are cared for by people who love them. Costs such as rent, electricity and food are also shared, which can make life more affordable. During difficult times, such as illness, unemployment or the death of a family member, a large family can provide emotional and financial support.

Children in joint families also benefit from contact with many relatives. They learn **family values, traditions and respect for elders** naturally, and they develop social skills by living with people of different ages. Elderly members feel needed and respected, and they are rarely lonely.

However, joint families also have disadvantages. Living with many people can lead to a **lack of privacy** and frequent conflicts, for example between parents and grandparents about how to raise children, or between relatives about money and household decisions. Young couples may feel that they cannot make their own choices. In some joint families, women carry a heavy burden of housework and have less freedom.

The nuclear family offers **independence and privacy**. Parents can make their own decisions about their home, finances and children's upbringing. Nuclear families are also more mobile; they can move more easily to another city or country for better jobs or education. Many people feel that a smaller family allows them to give more attention to each child.

On the other hand, nuclear families can be isolated. Without relatives nearby, parents must handle all childcare, housework and emergencies themselves, which can be stressful, especially when both parents work. Children may spend long hours alone or with paid carers, and they may have little contact with grandparents. Elderly parents, left behind in villages or living alone, may suffer from **loneliness**.

The shift towards nuclear families is mainly caused by urbanisation, economic changes and changing attitudes. As people move to cities for work, they often live in small flats that cannot accommodate large families. Young people also increasingly value independence and personal freedom.

In conclusion, both family types have their own strengths. Joint families provide support, care and strong traditions, while nuclear families offer privacy, independence and flexibility. Many modern families try to **combine the benefits** of both by living separately but staying closely connected through regular visits and communication.

### Caring for Elderly People

Thanks to better healthcare and living standards, people are living much longer than in the past. Many people now live into their eighties and nineties. While this is a great achievement, it also raises an important question: who should take care of elderly people? Some believe this is the duty of the family, while others think the government should be responsible.

In many cultures, including Bangladeshi culture, caring for elderly parents is seen as a **moral and religious duty**. Parents spend many years raising their children, so it seems natural that children should look after them in old age. Elderly people who live with their families often feel loved, respected and useful. They can share their experience, help with childcare and enjoy the company of their grandchildren, which is good for their mental and physical health.

However, families today face many difficulties in caring for elderly relatives. Many adults work long hours, and in families where both partners work, there may be no one at home during the day. Others have moved to cities or abroad for work, leaving their parents alone in villages. Small city flats may not have enough space for elderly parents. In addition, elderly people with serious health problems, such as dementia, heart disease or disability, may need **professional medical care** that family members are not trained to provide.

For these reasons, many people argue that the government must play a role. Governments can provide **pensions** so that elderly people have a basic income, free or affordable healthcare, and home-care services in which trained carers visit elderly people in their homes. For those who cannot live independently and have no family support, good-quality care homes are necessary.

Care homes are often criticised, especially in societies where family care is traditional. Some elderly people feel abandoned when they are sent to such homes, and poorly managed care homes may provide low-quality care. However, well-run care homes can offer professional medical support, social activities and company, which may be better than being alone at home.

The best approach is **shared responsibility**. Families should provide love, company and day-to-day support wherever possible. Governments should support families by offering pensions, healthcare, home-care services and financial help or paid leave for family carers. Employers can help by allowing flexible working hours for employees who care for elderly parents.

Society should also value elderly people more. Community centres, social clubs and volunteer programmes can keep older people active and connected, reducing loneliness and allowing them to share their skills and experience.

In conclusion, caring for the elderly is a responsibility that should be shared between families and the government. Families can give love and respect, while the state can provide financial security and professional services, ensuring that people can **grow old with dignity**.

### The Generation Gap and Time Together

The **generation gap** refers to the differences in values, attitudes, interests and lifestyles between older and younger generations. Every generation grows up in a different world, so some differences are natural. However, in recent decades, rapid changes in technology, education and society have made this gap wider, and many families now spend less time together than in the past.

There are several reasons why generations spend less time together. The first is the **busy modern lifestyle**. Parents often work long hours, while children spend much of their time at school, in private tuition and doing homework. Weekends are often filled with shopping, social events and extra classes, leaving little time for shared family activities.

The second reason is **technology**. In the past, families often gathered in the evening to talk, play games or watch the same television programme. Today, each family member may have their own smartphone, tablet or computer and spend their free time in separate digital worlds. Young people prefer social media and online games, while older people may not understand or enjoy these activities.

The third reason is **migration and urbanisation**. Many young people move to cities or abroad for education and work, leaving their parents and grandparents behind. Even when they live in the same city, family members may live in separate homes and meet only occasionally.

The generation gap can also be seen in values and opinions. Older people may have traditional views about marriage, careers, clothing and religion, while young people may be influenced by global culture and value personal freedom more. These differences can lead to misunderstandings and conflict.

Spending less time together has mostly **negative consequences**. Shared activities, such as family meals, conversations and trips, strengthen relationships and help family members understand and support each other. Children who spend time with their families tend to feel more secure and confident, and they learn values and social skills. Elderly people who are excluded from family life may feel lonely and unwanted.

On the other hand, some changes are not entirely negative. Young people today are often more independent, and technology can also connect generations, for example through video calls with grandparents who live far away. Young people can teach older relatives how to use smartphones, while older people can share their wisdom and experience.

To reduce the generation gap, families can make a conscious effort to spend **quality time** together, such as eating at least one meal a day together without phones, celebrating festivals together and taking part in activities that both generations enjoy. Open and respectful communication is essential, so that both sides listen to and learn from each other.

In conclusion, busy lifestyles, technology and migration have reduced the time that generations spend together, which is mainly a negative development. However, with effort and **mutual respect**, families can bridge the gap and keep strong relationships across generations.

### Grandparents and Grandchildren

The relationship between grandparents and grandchildren can be one of the most special bonds in a family. Grandparents often have more time and patience than busy parents, and they can offer love, wisdom and stability. However, in many modern societies, grandchildren rarely spend quality time with their grandparents, and this can have important consequences for both generations.

Grandparents play many valuable roles. They often help with **childcare**, especially when both parents work, providing loving and trustworthy care at no cost. They are also important **keepers of family history and culture**. Through stories, songs, recipes and traditions, grandparents pass on knowledge about the family's past and the community's way of life. Children who listen to their grandparents' stories, for example about village life or historical events, gain a sense of identity and belonging.

Grandparents can also offer emotional support. Children sometimes feel more comfortable talking to their grandparents than to their parents, because grandparents are often more relaxed and less strict. Their life experience allows them to give useful advice about friendship, work and difficult situations.

For grandparents, spending time with grandchildren brings joy and purpose. It keeps them mentally and physically active and reduces **loneliness**, which is a common problem among elderly people. Playing with children, helping with homework or teaching a skill can make them feel valued and needed.

However, modern lifestyles often keep the generations apart. Families may live in different cities or countries, and even when they live nearby, children's busy schedules of school, tuition and activities leave little free time. Many children also prefer screens to conversation, and some grandparents find it difficult to understand the interests of today's young people.

The consequences of this separation are significant. Grandparents may feel isolated and forgotten, which can harm their mental health. Children may lose touch with their family history, traditional values and even their mother tongue or regional dialect. They may also miss the patience, unconditional love and practical wisdom that grandparents can offer. Over time, the **generation gap** may widen, making it harder for different generations to understand each other.

There are several ways to strengthen this relationship. Families can arrange regular visits, especially during holidays and festivals, and use video calls to stay in touch when they live far apart. Grandparents and grandchildren can share activities that both enjoy, such as cooking, gardening, telling stories or playing simple games. Grandchildren can teach their grandparents how to use technology, which helps both sides connect.

In conclusion, grandparents bring love, wisdom and cultural continuity to children's lives, while grandchildren bring joy and purpose to grandparents. When these generations are separated, both lose something valuable. Families should therefore make an effort to **keep these bonds strong**.

### Parenting: Discipline versus Nurturing

Raising children is one of the most important responsibilities in life. Parents must help their children grow into healthy, happy and responsible adults. However, people disagree about the main role of parents. Some believe that parents should mainly **discipline** their children and teach them right from wrong, while others believe that their main role is to **nurture** their children by providing love, care and a safe environment.

Those who emphasise discipline argue that children need **clear rules and boundaries**. Without discipline, children may become selfish, rude or uncontrollable, and they may fail to respect teachers, elders and the law. Parents are children's first teachers of moral values such as honesty, kindness and responsibility. When parents correct bad behaviour consistently, children learn that actions have consequences, which helps them develop **self-control**. Routines, such as fixed times for homework and sleep, also prepare children for the structure of school and working life.

On the other hand, those who emphasise nurturing argue that love and security are the foundation of healthy development. Children who feel loved and safe tend to be more confident, curious and emotionally stable. They are better able to form good relationships, cope with stress and recover from difficulties. A warm, supportive home gives children the confidence to try new things and learn from mistakes.

Discipline without love can be harmful. Very strict parents who use shouting, threats or physical punishment may make children fearful, anxious or rebellious. Such children may obey out of fear rather than understanding, and they may hide problems from their parents instead of asking for help. In some cases, harsh punishment can lead to aggressive behaviour.

At the same time, love without any rules can also cause problems. Parents who give their children everything they want and never say no may raise children who struggle to accept limits, share with others or work hard.

Most experts agree that the best approach combines both: **firm but loving parenting**. Parents should set clear, reasonable rules and explain the reasons behind them, while showing warmth, patience and respect. Instead of harsh punishment, they can use positive methods, such as praising good behaviour, setting a good example and using logical consequences, like losing screen time after breaking a rule.

Parents also teach most powerfully through their own behaviour. Children copy what they see. Parents who are honest, kind and respectful are more likely to raise children with the same values than parents who only give lectures.

In conclusion, both discipline and nurturing are essential parts of parenting. Discipline teaches children right from wrong, while nurturing gives them the security and confidence to grow. The most effective parents **combine clear rules with love**, raising children who are both responsible and emotionally healthy.

### Working Parents and Childcare

In many modern families, both parents work outside the home. This change has been driven by rising living costs, women's greater access to education and employment, and changing attitudes towards gender roles. While dual-income families have many benefits, one consequence is that children often spend less time with their parents.

There are several reasons why both parents work. Firstly, **the cost of living** has risen sharply, especially in cities. Housing, education, healthcare and transport are expensive, and many families need two incomes simply to meet their basic needs. Secondly, more women are now highly educated and want to use their skills and build careers. Thirdly, society increasingly accepts and values women's employment, and many families see financial independence for both partners as important.

Dual-income families enjoy several advantages. They usually have a **higher standard of living**, which allows them to provide better education, healthcare and opportunities for their children. Working mothers can be positive **role models**, showing children, especially daughters, that women can be successful professionals. Women's employment also gives them financial independence and a greater voice in family decisions.

However, when both parents work long hours, children may receive **less attention and supervision**. Parents may come home tired and have little energy to talk, play or help with homework. Young children may spend long hours with domestic helpers or in childcare centres, while older children may come home to an empty house. Without supervision, some children may spend too much time on screens, eat unhealthy food or fall into bad company.

Children may also feel emotionally neglected if they rarely have meaningful time with their parents. Family meals, conversations and shared activities may become rare. This can weaken the parent–child relationship and make it harder for parents to notice when their children are facing problems at school or with friends.

Parents themselves can suffer from stress as they try to balance work and family responsibilities. Working mothers in particular often face a **double burden**, doing paid work during the day and most of the housework and childcare in the evening.

There are several ways to reduce these problems. Employers can offer **flexible working hours**, part-time options and the possibility of working from home. Governments can provide affordable, high-quality childcare and paid parental leave for both mothers and fathers. Within the family, fathers and mothers should share housework and childcare more equally. Most importantly, parents can focus on the **quality of time** they spend with their children, for example by eating together, talking about their day and spending weekends together.

In conclusion, both parents working brings financial security and equality, but it can reduce the time and attention children receive. With supportive employers, government policies and a fair sharing of responsibilities at home, families can enjoy the benefits of two incomes while still giving children the care they need.

### Children's Freedom and Independence

Children today often have more freedom than children in previous generations. They have their own mobile phones, access to the internet, pocket money and, in some cases, the freedom to choose their own activities, friends and clothes. In the past, children were usually expected to obey their parents without question. Whether this increased freedom is a positive or negative development is a common subject of debate.

There are clear benefits to giving children more freedom. When children are allowed to make their own decisions, they develop **independence, confidence and responsibility**. A child who chooses their own hobbies, manages a small amount of money or travels to school alone learns practical life skills and how to deal with problems. These abilities are essential in adulthood, when they will have to make important decisions about education, careers and relationships.

Freedom can also encourage **creativity and self-expression**. Children who are allowed to explore their interests, ask questions and share their opinions often become more curious and innovative. Modern parents tend to listen more to their children's views, which can create closer and more open relationships based on trust rather than fear.

However, too much freedom can be dangerous. Children do not always have the maturity to understand risks. With unrestricted access to the internet, they may be exposed to violent or inappropriate content, online bullying or strangers with bad intentions. Without guidance, some children may spend excessive time on screens, neglect their studies or develop unhealthy habits.

Too much freedom may also weaken **respect and discipline**. Children who are rarely told "no" may struggle to accept rules at school or in society, and some may become selfish or disrespectful towards parents, teachers and elders. Teenagers who are allowed to go out without supervision may fall under the influence of bad company, which can lead to smoking, drug use or crime.

The level of freedom should depend on a child's **age and maturity**. Young children need close supervision and clear rules, while teenagers can gradually be given more responsibility as they show that they can handle it. This step-by-step approach helps children learn from small mistakes in a safe environment.

Parents play a vital role in balancing freedom and guidance. They can set clear limits, such as screen-time rules and curfews, explain the reasons behind these rules and talk openly with their children about online safety and other risks. Instead of simply controlling their children, parents should aim to build trust, so that children feel comfortable asking for advice.

In conclusion, greater freedom for children can be a positive development, because it builds independence, confidence and creativity. However, freedom without guidance can expose children to serious risks. The best approach is **freedom with responsibility**, gradually increased as children grow older and more mature.

### Parental Pressure and Children's Success

Every parent wants their children to succeed. However, in many societies today, parents put a great deal of pressure on their children to achieve top grades, win competitions and enter prestigious universities and careers. In Bangladesh and other parts of Asia, children often attend school during the day and private tuition in the evening, with little time for play or rest. While high expectations can motivate children, excessive pressure can cause serious harm.

There are several reasons why parents push their children so hard. The most important is **intense competition**. Places at the best schools, universities and jobs are limited, and parents fear that their children will be left behind if they do not perform exceptionally well. Many parents believe that academic success is the only route to financial security and social status, particularly in professions such as medicine and engineering.

**Social comparison** is another factor. Parents may compare their children's results with those of relatives, neighbours and friends, and they may feel embarrassed if their child performs worse. Some parents also want their children to achieve the dreams that they themselves could not achieve, or to make up for the hardships that they experienced.

Moderate expectations can have positive effects. Children whose parents show interest in their education and encourage them to work hard often develop **good study habits, discipline and ambition**. Parental support can help children reach their full potential.

However, **excessive pressure** has many negative consequences. Children who are constantly pushed may suffer from stress, anxiety, sleep problems and depression. They may lose their childhood, with no time for play, hobbies, friends or rest, which are important for healthy development. Some children develop a fear of failure and lose their natural curiosity and love of learning, studying only to avoid their parents' disappointment. In extreme cases, academic pressure has been linked to serious mental health problems among young people.

Pressure can also damage the relationship between parents and children. Children who feel that they are valued only for their results may become distant, rebellious or dishonest, hiding bad marks out of fear. They may also be pushed into careers that do not suit their interests or abilities, leading to unhappiness later in life.

The role of parents should be to **support rather than control**. They can encourage effort and progress rather than focusing only on grades, help children discover their own interests and strengths, and accept that every child is different. Parents should also make sure that children have time for rest, play and family life. Schools and society can help by valuing a wider range of talents, such as creativity, sports, arts and practical skills.

In conclusion, parental pressure is mainly caused by competition and social expectations. While encouragement can help children succeed, excessive pressure harms their mental health and happiness. A **balanced approach**, combining high but realistic expectations with love and support, is the best way to help children succeed.

### Technology, Television and Family Life

Technology has transformed family life in the past few decades. Most homes now have televisions, smartphones, tablets and internet access, and many family members spend several hours a day using screens. Technology brings many benefits, but it has also changed how families communicate and spend time together.

One major benefit is that technology helps families **stay connected**. Family members who live in different cities or countries can talk and see each other every day through video calls and messaging apps. For many Bangladeshi families with relatives working abroad, this has made separation much easier to bear. Grandparents can watch their grandchildren grow up, even from far away.

Television and the internet can also be **educational**. Well-made documentaries, educational programmes and learning apps can teach children about science, nature, history and languages in an interesting way. Some people even suggest that children should be encouraged to watch television regularly at home and at school because it can be an effective learning tool. Families can also enjoy watching films or programmes together, which can create shared experiences and topics for conversation.

However, technology can reduce the **quality of family interaction**. It is now common to see family members sitting in the same room, each focused on their own screen. Meals may be eaten in silence while everyone checks their phones. Parents who are constantly busy with work emails or social media may give less attention to their children, and children who spend hours online may withdraw from family conversations.

Excessive screen time is also harmful to children's health and development. Children who watch a lot of television or play video games often spend less time playing outside, reading or talking with others, which can affect their physical fitness, language development and social skills. Television also exposes children to advertisements and content that may not be suitable for their age. Although some programmes are educational, much television is designed mainly for entertainment, and watching passively does not replace active learning, play and conversation.

Another concern is that technology can increase the **generation gap**. Young people who grow up with digital technology may communicate in ways that older relatives do not understand, and grandparents who cannot use smartphones may feel excluded from family conversations that take place online.

Families can enjoy the benefits of technology while limiting its harms. Parents can set **screen-free times**, such as during meals and before bedtime, and choose good-quality educational content for young children. Watching programmes together and discussing them can turn screen time into family time. Parents should also set a good example by limiting their own phone use.

In conclusion, technology helps families stay connected and can support learning, but excessive use can weaken family relationships and harm children's development. With sensible rules and **conscious use**, technology can bring families closer rather than pushing them apart.

### Changing Gender Roles, Marriage and Having Children

Family life has been shaped for centuries by traditional gender roles, in which men worked outside the home and earned money, while women stayed at home, cooked, cleaned and raised children. In recent decades, these roles have changed significantly in many societies. Women's education and employment have increased, attitudes towards marriage have changed, and more couples are making different choices about whether and when to have children.

One of the biggest changes is that more **women are working** outside the home. In Bangladesh, for example, millions of women work in the garment industry, and increasing numbers are doctors, teachers, engineers and business owners. Women's employment has improved family incomes and given many women financial independence and greater confidence. At the same time, more men are sharing housework and childcare, although in many families women still do most of this work.

Attitudes towards **marriage** are also changing. In many countries, people are getting married later, focusing first on education and careers. In some societies, especially in the West, more people choose not to marry at all, or to live together without marriage. Some argue that this is because women can now earn their own income and no longer need the financial security that marriage once provided. Others believe it reflects a wider change in values, with greater emphasis on personal freedom and choice.

Another trend, particularly in Western countries, is the increasing number of **couples who choose not to have children**. Such couples often have more time and money for their careers, hobbies, travel and each other. They may also avoid the stress and costs of raising children. However, they may miss the joy and fulfilment that many people find in parenthood, and they may lack family support in old age. At a national level, low birth rates can lead to an ageing population and fewer workers to support pensions and healthcare.

These changes have both positive and negative effects on family life. On the positive side, greater **gender equality** allows both men and women to develop their abilities and share responsibilities. Children benefit from seeing both parents as capable and caring. Marriages based on choice and equality may be more satisfying.

On the negative side, some people worry that changing roles have weakened traditional family structures. When both parents work, children may receive less attention, and the pressure to balance work and family can create stress and conflict. Later marriage and fewer children may also reduce the size and support network of families.

In conclusion, gender roles, marriage and attitudes towards having children have changed significantly, mainly because of women's education and employment and changing social values. While these changes bring challenges, they have also created **more equal and flexible families**, in which both partners can contribute to work and family life.

### City or Countryside: Where to Raise Children

Parents often wonder where it is better for children to grow up: in a busy city or in the quiet countryside. Each environment offers different advantages and disadvantages, and the answer may depend on a family's values, finances and opportunities.

Cities offer many **opportunities** for children. The best schools, colleges, libraries, hospitals and sports facilities are usually located in urban areas. Children can join a wide range of activities, such as music lessons, coding clubs, sports teams and language classes, which help them discover and develop their talents. Cities also expose children to people from different backgrounds and cultures, which can make them more open-minded and confident. In addition, good healthcare is easier to access in an emergency.

However, city life also has disadvantages. Many cities, such as Dhaka, suffer from **air pollution, traffic congestion and noise**, which can harm children's health. Families often live in small flats with little space to play, and there may be few safe parks or playgrounds. Because of traffic and safety concerns, parents may keep their children indoors, where they spend more time on screens. Cities can also be expensive, and crime and bad influences may be more common.

The countryside offers a **healthier and more natural environment**. Children can breathe cleaner air, play freely in open fields, climb trees and swim in ponds, which helps them stay active and develop physically. They can learn about nature, farming and animals through direct experience. Rural communities are often close-knit; neighbours know each other, and children grow up surrounded by relatives and a strong sense of community.

On the other hand, rural areas often have **fewer opportunities**. Schools may lack qualified teachers, laboratories and libraries, and there may be few extracurricular activities. Hospitals and specialist doctors may be far away. Young people may have limited exposure to new ideas and career options, and many eventually need to move to cities for higher education and work.

Some families try to combine the benefits of both by living in smaller towns or suburbs, where there is more space and less pollution than in big cities but still good access to schools and services. Others raise children in cities but make sure they spend holidays in their village homes, where they can enjoy nature and stay connected to their roots.

Governments can reduce the differences between urban and rural childhoods by improving schools, healthcare and internet access in villages, and by creating more parks and safe green spaces in cities.

In conclusion, cities offer better education, services and opportunities, while the countryside provides a healthier environment and stronger community ties. The best place to raise children depends on each family's priorities, but children ideally need **both opportunities and a healthy environment** to grow up happily.

### Family Values, Personality and Success

What makes a person successful in life? Some people believe that the values children learn from their parents and families, such as honesty, hard work and respect, have more influence on their future than the knowledge and skills they learn at school. Others argue that formal education is the key to success. Related to this is the debate about whether personality and intelligence are fixed at birth or shaped by upbringing and experience.

Family values play a powerful role in shaping a person's character. From an early age, children learn from their parents how to behave, how to treat others and how to respond to challenges. A child who grows up in a family that values **hard work, honesty and perseverance** is likely to carry these qualities into school, work and relationships. These values often determine whether a person keeps trying after failure, behaves ethically and earns the trust of others, all of which are essential for long-term success.

Many successful people come from families with limited wealth or education but strong values. Parents who encourage their children, set high standards and show by their own example the importance of effort can inspire children to overcome difficulties. On the other hand, children from wealthy families who lack discipline and good values may waste their opportunities.

However, **school education** is also extremely important. Schools provide the knowledge and qualifications that are required for most careers, such as reading, writing, mathematics, science and technology. Teachers can also reinforce good values and help children develop social skills, teamwork and critical thinking. For children whose families cannot provide good guidance, school may be the most important positive influence in their lives.

The debate about **nature and nurture** is also relevant. Some people believe that intelligence and personality are largely determined by genes, so that people are born clever, shy or confident. While genetics certainly play a role, everyday experience shows that the environment, including family, education and life experiences, has a strong influence. People can improve their abilities through learning and practice, and many people change aspects of their personality as they grow older and face new challenges.

Similarly, success in life depends on many factors besides academic achievement. Some people who did poorly at school later become very successful because of their determination, creativity, communication skills or business sense. This suggests that qualities developed at home and through experience, such as ambition and resilience, can be just as important as academic results.

In conclusion, both family values and school education contribute to a person's success. Education provides knowledge and opportunities, while family values build the character that allows people to use those opportunities well. Because personality and intelligence can develop over time, **both upbringing and learning** matter greatly.

**Formula:** [long noun phrase] + verb + object
*একটাই clause, কিন্তু subject-টা বড় ও তথ্যপূর্ণ।*

- The rapid move from joint families to nuclear families in many Asian cities **has changed** the role of grandparents.
- Regular meals with all family members around the table **help** children feel secure and valued.

#### 2. Compound with *and / but / so / yet*

- Joint families offer support, **but** they can lack privacy.
- Both parents work long hours, **so** children often come home to an empty house.

#### 3. Compound with semicolon + linker

- Children need freedom**; however,** they also need clear rules.
- Many young people move abroad**; as a result,** their parents grow old alone.

#### 4. Complex — concession (*Although / Even though / While*)

- **Although** care homes provide medical support, many elderly people feel lonely there.
- **Even though** parents want the best for their children, too much pressure can harm them.

#### 5. Complex — reason (*Because / Since / As*)

- **Since** grandparents have more free time, they can give children patient attention.
- **Because** city flats are small, many families cannot live with their elderly parents.

#### 6. Complex — condition (*If / Unless / Provided that*)

- **If** parents set a good example, children **are likely to** follow it.
- **Unless** families make time for each other, the generation gap **will** keep growing.

#### 7. Relative clause (*which / who / where / whose*)

- Children **whose** parents read to them develop better language skills.
- Many families now live in small city flats, **where** there is no room for grandparents.

#### 8. Compound-complex

**Formula:** Although/Because/If + clause, Clause A, and/but Clause B

- **Although** both parents work, they can still eat dinner together, **and** this keeps the family close.
- **If** children have too much freedom, they may fall into bad company, **but** too little freedom makes them dependent.

#### 9. Participle phrase

- **Raised** by loving grandparents, many children grow up with strong values.
- **Living** far from their families, migrant workers often feel lonely.

#### 10. *Not only … but also …*

- Grandparents **not only** look after children **but also** pass on family traditions.
- Excessive pressure **not only** causes stress **but also** destroys children's love of learning.

#### 11. *The more …, the more …*

- **The more** time families spend on screens, **the less** they talk to each other.
- **The earlier** children learn responsibility, **the more** independent they become.

#### 12. Cleft sentence (emphasis)

- **It is** the quality, not the quantity, of family time **that** matters most.
- **What** children need most **is** love and attention from their parents.

#### 13. Impersonal passive

- **It is often said that** children are the future of a nation.
- **It is widely believed that** family values shape a person's character.

#### 14. Contrasting views

- **While some people prefer** the independence of nuclear families, **others value** the support of joint families.
- **While** parents provide love, **schools** provide knowledge and skills.

#### 15. Result / consequence

- Both parents work full-time, **which means that** children spend long hours with domestic helpers.
- Families have become smaller. **As a result,** elderly people often live alone.

অন্যদিকে, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে লালনপালনই বাবা-মায়ের প্রধান দায়িত্ব, কারণ যে শিশুরা নিজেদের ভালোবাসার ও নিরাপদ মনে করে, তারা বেশি আত্মবিশ্বাসী হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> পরিবার যখন উষ্ণ ও স্থিতিশীল হয়, তখন শিশুরা শেখার ও বন্ধু তৈরির মানসিক শক্তি (emotional strength) পায়, এবং তারা মানসিক চাপ ভালোভাবে সামলাতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, যে কিশোর বাড়িতে নিরাপদ বোধ করে, সে সাহায্য চাওয়ার বদলে বাবা-মায়ের কাছ থেকে সমস্যা লুকায় না, যা পরিবারকে শুরুতেই সমস্যার সমাধান করতে সাহায্য করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে শিশুদের কঠোরভাবে শোধরানো দরকার, ভালোবাসাহীন শাস্তি তাদের ভীত বা এমনকি বিদ্রোহী করে তুলতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে ভালোবাসা ও নিরাপত্তাই বাবা-মায়ের দেওয়া সবচেয়ে গুরুত্বপূর্ণ জিনিস। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Conclusion (35–45 words)

পরিশেষে, বাবা-মায়ের প্রধান কাজ সন্তানকে শৃঙ্খলা শেখানো নাকি স্নেহ দিয়ে বড় করা, এ নিয়ে মানুষের মধ্যে ভিন্ন ভিন্ন মত আছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও স্পষ্ট নিয়ম শিশুদের আচরণ শেখায়, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে লালনপালন বেশি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ, কারণ ভালোবাসা ও নিরাপত্তা তরুণদের আত্মবিশ্বাস দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Introduction (30–40 words)

একটি পরিবারের ভিন্ন ভিন্ন প্রজন্মের সদস্যরা এখন যেন একসাথে কম কাজে অংশ নেন। <span class="tag">Simple</span> এর মূল কারণ হলো আধুনিক জীবন ব্যস্ত এবং প্রযুক্তি মানুষকে আলাদা করে দেয়, এবং আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে এটি একটি নেতিবাচক পরিবর্তন। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — Q1: List block (90–120 words)

এর একটি প্রধান কারণ হলো ব্যস্ত আধুনিক জীবনধারা (busy modern lifestyle)। <span class="tag">Simple</span> বাবা-মা প্রায়ই দীর্ঘ সময় কাজ করেন, অন্যদিকে সন্তানেরা দিনের বেশিরভাগ সময় স্কুলে, প্রাইভেট টিউশনে ও বাড়ির কাজে কাটায়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, অফিস থেকে দেরিতে বাড়ি ফেরা একজন বাবা হয়তো দেখেন যে তার ছেলে তখনও টিউশনে, এবং এর মানে হলো তারা খুব কমই একসাথে খাবার খান। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> আরেকটি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ কারণ হলো প্রযুক্তি, যা পরিবারগুলোর সন্ধ্যা কাটানোর ধরন বদলে দিয়েছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আগে আত্মীয়রা একসাথে বসে একই টেলিভিশন অনুষ্ঠান দেখতেন, অথচ এখন প্রত্যেক সদস্য হয়তো অবসর সময় নিজের আলাদা ডিজিটাল জগতে (digital world) কাটান। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে একটি পরিবার একই বাড়িতে থেকেও একসাথে খুব কম সময় কাটাতে পারে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — Q2: Opinion block (90–120 words)

এই প্রবণতা ইতিবাচক না নেতিবাচক — এ প্রশ্নে আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে এটি ক্ষতিকর, কারণ একসাথে কাজ করলে পারিবারিক সম্পর্ক মজবুত হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আত্মীয়রা যখন একসাথে খান, কথা বলেন ও বেড়াতে যান, তখন তারা একে অপরকে বুঝতে ও পাশে দাঁড়াতে শেখেন, এবং শিশুরা আরও নিরাপদ ও আত্মবিশ্বাসী বোধ করে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> যেমন, যে কিশোর প্রতি সন্ধ্যায় রাতের খাবারের সময় বাবা-মায়ের সাথে কথা বলে, তার কোনো সমস্যায় সাহায্য চাওয়ার সম্ভাবনা বেশি, যা সমস্যাটিকে গুরুতর হয়ে ওঠা থেকে থামাতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে video call প্রজন্মগুলোকে যুক্ত রাখতে পারে, একটি ছোট call একসাথে কাটানো সময়ের ঘনিষ্ঠতার জায়গা নিতে পারে না। <span class="tag">Complex</span> তাই পারিবারিক সময় এভাবে হারিয়ে যাওয়া স্পষ্টতই একটি নেতিবাচক পরিবর্তন। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Conclusion (30–40 words)

পরিশেষে, পরিবারগুলো একসাথে কম সময় কাটায়, কারণ দৈনন্দিন সময়সূচি ঠাসা এবং ডিজিটাল যন্ত্র আত্মীয়দের আলাদা করে রাখে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে এই পরিবর্তন ক্ষতিকর, কারণ এটি মানুষের জীবনের সবচেয়ে ঘনিষ্ঠ বন্ধনগুলো দুর্বল করে দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

</div>

**Common mistakes in family essays (এগুলো এড়িয়ে চলো):**

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- প্রশ্ন শুধু সুবিধা-অসুবিধা চায় (Type 1): কোনো রায় বা "দুটোর সুবিধা মিলিয়ে নেওয়া"র পরামর্শ নয়।
- Body 1 (ঠিক 7টি বাক্য): nuclear family-র 2টি সুবিধা (গোপনীয়তা ও স্বাধীন সিদ্ধান্ত; প্রতিটি সন্তানের প্রতি বেশি মনোযোগ); topic sentence-এ "nuclear family" লেখো।
- Body 2 (ঠিক 7টি বাক্য): 2টি অসুবিধা (শিশুর দেখাশোনায় সাহায্য নেই; বয়স্কদের একাকীত্ব) ও একটি বাস্তব উদাহরণ; শেষ বাক্য "Overall, these…"।
- Although ও While বাক্যে আগে অসুবিধা, comma-র পরে সুবিধা, but ছাড়া।

</div>

**Try to use:** nuclear family · privacy and independence · move for work · mutual support · childcare · loneliness · pass on traditions · stay closely connected

**Sentence target:** use *While…, …* once to contrast the two family types and *which means that…* once.

</div>

### Homework 2 — Double Question

<div class="question">Most modern families have both parents working, and as a result, children spend less and less time with their parents. What is the reason for this? What problems can this cause?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · FAM-07*

**Essay type:** Double Question — List + List · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 = Q1 (List block) 90–120 · BP2 = Q2 (List block) 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** Working Parents & Childcare; Changing Gender Roles; Technology & Family Life

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- দুটি প্রশ্নই কিছুর নাম জানতে চায়, তাই দুটিই List block; essay-র কোথাও "I" নয়।
- BP1: 2টি কারণ (জীবনযাত্রার খরচ; নারীর শিক্ষা ও ক্যারিয়ার) — উদাহরণ: ঢাকায় এক বেতনে বাসাভাড়া ও স্কুলের বেতন দেওয়া কঠিন।
- BP2: 2টি সমস্যা (শিশুর তত্ত্বাবধানের অভাব, যেমন বিকেলে খালি বাসায় একা থাকা শিশু; বাবা-মায়ের, বিশেষ করে মায়ের, double burden)।
- প্রশ্নে সমাধান চাওয়া হয়নি, তাই উপসংহারে কোনো সমাধান নয়। Introduction: paraphrase + [উত্তর 1], and [উত্তর 2]। Conclusion-এ দুটি উত্তর নতুন শব্দে, নতুন idea নয়।

</div>

**Try to use:** dual-income families · the cost of living · a higher standard of living · parental supervision · emotionally neglected · the double burden · quality time · affordable childcare

**Sentence target:** use *not only… but also…* once and *Because…, …* once.

</div>

### Homework 3 — Advantages–Disadvantages

<div class="question">Children these days have more freedom than in previous generations. Do you think this is a positive or negative development?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · FAM-12 · ★ children's upbringing*

**Essay type:** Advantages–Disadvantages, Type 3 (positive or negative?) · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 330–360 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 40–50 · BP1 advantages 120–135 · BP2 disadvantages 120–135 · Conclusion 35–45<br>
**Use ideas from:** Children's Freedom & Independence; Parenting; Technology & Family Life

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- একটিই রায় দাও: positive অথবা negative। "mostly positive, if guided" লিখবে না।
- Body 1 (ঠিক 7টি বাক্য): স্বাধীনতার 2টি সুবিধা (আত্মবিশ্বাস, সিদ্ধান্ত নেওয়ার দক্ষতা)।
- Body 2 (ঠিক 7টি বাক্য): 2টি ঝুঁকি (অনলাইন বিপদ, খারাপ সঙ্গ) ও একটি উদাহরণ; সুবিধার পক্ষে হলে শেষ বাক্য "Although these…"।
- উপসংহারে নতুন পরামর্শ ("freedom with responsibility") নয়: While বাক্য + "Overall, I believe…"।

</div>

**Try to use:** independence and confidence · make their own decisions · life skills · inappropriate content · bad company · set clear limits · age and maturity · freedom with responsibility

**Sentence target:** use *The more…, the more…* once and one relative clause with *whose*.

</div>

### Homework 4 — Double Question

<div class="question">In the past, the older generation were a source of knowledge and experience. Nowadays, the older generation have little understanding or experience of the modern world. Has this had any negative impact on the way the elderly are treated by society and the family? What could be done to solve this situation?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · FAM-09 · ★ care for elderly people*

**Essay type:** Double Question — Opinion + List · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 = Q1 (Opinion block) 90–120 · BP2 = Q2 (List block) 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** Caring for Elderly People; Generation Gap; Grandparents & Grandchildren

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- Q1 "Has this had any negative impact?" পক্ষ বেছে নিতে বলে, তাই Opinion block: হ্যাঁ — বয়স্কদের পরামর্শ উপেক্ষিত হয়। উদাহরণ: স্মার্টফোন না জানায় পারিবারিক group chat থেকে বাদ পড়া দাদা।
- Opinion block-এর Response: Although some people argue that families still respect their elders, [তোমার পক্ষ]।
- Q2 List block: 2টি সমাধান (তরুণেরা প্রযুক্তি শেখাবে, বয়স্করা অভিজ্ঞতা; কমিউনিটি সেন্টার ও ক্লাব); Body 2-তে "I" নয়।
- Introduction: paraphrase + [উত্তর 1], and [উত্তর 2]। Conclusion-এ দুটি উত্তর নতুন শব্দে, নতুন idea নয়।

</div>

**Try to use:** the older generation · a source of wisdom and experience · feel excluded / unwanted · the generation gap · digital skills · teach each other · show respect for · grow old with dignity

**Sentence target:** use one cleft sentence (*It is… that…*) and *If…, …will…* once.

</div>
