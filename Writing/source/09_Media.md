## Part 1 — Idea Generation Flow {: .part}

<div class="flow">Media → Advertising &amp; Children → Consumerism &amp; Buying What We Don't Need → Fake News &amp; Misinformation → News Values: Bad News &amp; Objectivity → Media Responsibility &amp; Press Freedom → Celebrities, Influencers &amp; Role Models → Privacy of Famous People → Social Media vs Traditional News → Television &amp; Children's Behaviour → Films, Cinema &amp; Streaming → Books, E-books &amp; Reading → Sponsorship &amp; Marketing</div>

When you see **any** media or advertising question, run through this chain from left to right and ask: *"Does this question touch this area?"* Then **stop at the 2 links that fit the question best**. Those 2 links become your two body paragraphs.

**Memory trick — test every media question with T-I-M: Truth, Influence, Money**

| Test | Question to ask yourself | Example (advertising to children) |
|---|---|---|
| **T — Truth** | Is the information honest, accurate and fair? | adverts exaggerate; children cannot tell fact from persuasion |
| **I — Influence** | How does it affect people's behaviour, health or values? | children want junk food and toys; family arguments |
| **M — Money** | Who earns money, and does profit come before people? | companies and TV channels profit from children's attention |

Almost every media essay can use two of these three tests: one for each body paragraph.

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Example:** *"Famous people should have no right to privacy."*

T → the press often publishes rumours and edited photos ✔ — I → harassment harms celebrities' mental health and families ✔ — M → newspapers sell more copies with gossip ✔.
Chain links: **Privacy of Famous People** + **Media Responsibility**.

</div>

## Part 2 — Exam-Hall Brainstorming System {: .part}

### 2A. The 5-Minute Plan

| Minute | What to do | What you write on the question paper |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | **Decode the question.** Underline the topic words and the instruction words. Decide which of your five essay guides it uses (Part 2G). | e.g. *adverts / children / not allowed / your opinion → Agree / Disagree guide* |
| 1–3 | **T-I-M grid.** Truth, Influence, Money — one or two notes each. | 4–6 one-line notes |
| 3–4 | **Choose the 2 strongest ideas.** Add one reason and one example to each. | Idea 1 + why + example; Idea 2 + why + example |
| 4–5 | **Decide your position (one clear side, only if the question asks) and use the guide's word plan.** | Thesis / BP1 / BP2 / Conclusion in 4 lines |

### 2E. How One Idea Becomes a Full Paragraph (PEEL)

**P**oint → **E**xplain (why? how?) → **E**xample → **L**ink / Result

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### বাংলায় চিন্তা (Bangla thinking)

**Point:** ভুয়া খবর সমাজের জন্য একটি বড় হুমকি।
**Explain:** কারণ সোশ্যাল মিডিয়ায় কোনো তথ্য যাচাই না করেই কয়েক মিনিটে হাজার হাজার মানুষের কাছে ছড়িয়ে পড়ে।
**Example:** যেমন, কোনো এলাকা নিয়ে একটি মিথ্যা গুজব আতঙ্ক বা এমনকি সহিংসতাও সৃষ্টি করতে পারে।
**Link/Result:** ফলে মানুষ গণমাধ্যমের ওপর আস্থা হারায় এবং সমাজে বিভেদ বাড়ে।

</div>

**The same idea in English (one paragraph):**

> **Point:** Fake news is a serious threat to society.
> **Explain:** On social media, information can reach thousands of people within minutes without being checked.
> **Example:** For example, a false rumour about a particular area can cause panic or even violence.
> **Link:** As a result, people lose trust in the media, and divisions in society grow deeper.

Count it: about **50 words** from one idea. Add a second explanation (for example, why people believe and share false stories) and a second example to reach the **100–120 words** that one body paragraph needs.

### 2G. Essay-Type Paragraph Plans (your five essay guides)

| Essay type (guide) | Introduction | Body Paragraph 1 | Body Paragraph 2 | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Agree / Disagree** (*to what extent? / do you agree?*) | **35–50:** paraphrase + *I completely agree/disagree with this view because* Idea 1 and Idea 2. Never "partly". | **100–120:** Idea 1 — P E E X L | **100–120:** Idea 2 — P E X R L (*Although some people argue that …, [my side]*) | **35–55:** *In conclusion, I firmly believe that …* + both ideas in new words |
| **Discuss Both Views** (*some think X, others think Y*) | **35–45:** *While some people argue that [other view], others believe that [my view]. I completely support the latter view because …* | **90–120:** the view you do **not** support, no "I" — P E E X L | **90–120:** **your view** — P E X R L | **35–45:** *In conclusion, people hold different views on whether … While …, I firmly believe that …* |
| **Advantages – Disadvantages** | **40–50:** paraphrase + *Although* [side you do not support], [side you support] + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **30–40**, 2 sentences) | **120–135:** advantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **120–135:** disadvantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **35–45:** *While …* + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **25–35**) |
| **Cause/Problem + Solution** | **30–40:** paraphrase + *This is mainly because C1 and C2, but S1 and S2 can tackle this problem.* No opinion. | **90–120:** 2 causes or problems — P E X P E L | **90–120:** 2 solutions, each fixing one cause (WHO + WHAT + HOW) | **30–40:** causes + solutions in new words |
| **Double Question** (*two questions*) | **30–40:** paraphrase + *[Answer 1], and [answer 2].* | **90–120:** answer to Q1 — List block (P E X P E L) or Opinion block (P E X R L) | **90–120:** answer to Q2 — List or Opinion block | **30–40:** *In conclusion,* answer 1 + answer 2 in new words |

*Full rules: `Writing/IELTS_*_Essay_Guide.md`. Questions asking only for solutions or only for problems use the Cause/Problem + Solution guide, Section 14: one idea in each body paragraph (P E E X L), no opinion.*

## Part 3 — Category-Wise Free Writing {: .part}

Each passage below is an **idea store**. Read it slowly, notice how each idea is explained and supported with an example, and pay attention to the **bold collocations**. After each passage, try to say 4–5 of its ideas aloud in your own words.

### Advertising and Children

Children today are exposed to more advertising than any previous generation. They see adverts on television, on YouTube, in mobile games, on social media and even in schools. Companies know that children influence what their families buy, and that habits formed in childhood can last a lifetime. For this reason, many people believe that advertising aimed at children should be strictly controlled or even banned.

The main concern is that children are **especially vulnerable** to advertising. Young children cannot easily distinguish between programmes and adverts, and they often do not understand that the purpose of an advert is to persuade them to buy something. They tend to believe what they see, so they may think that a toy will make them popular or that a snack will make them strong. As a result, they may **pester their parents** to buy products, which can lead to arguments and financial pressure, especially in low-income families.

Food advertising is a particular problem. Many adverts aimed at children promote fast food, sweets, sugary cereals and soft drinks, often using cartoon characters, celebrities and free toys to make them more attractive. These products are high in sugar, salt and fat, and regular consumption contributes to **childhood obesity**, tooth decay and other health problems. Some argue that such advertising is partly responsible for the rise in overweight children in many countries.

Advertising can also shape children's values. Constant messages that happiness comes from owning the latest toy, gadget or brand can encourage **materialism**. Children may judge themselves and others by what they own, and they may feel unhappy or left out if their families cannot afford advertised products.

On the other hand, some people argue that a complete ban would be unfair and unrealistic. Advertising provides important income for television channels and online platforms, which helps to fund children's programmes. Some adverts provide useful information about books, educational games or healthy products. In addition, in a world where children use the internet freely, it would be very difficult to block all advertising.

Many countries have chosen a middle path. They ban or restrict adverts for unhealthy food during children's programmes, forbid the use of cartoon characters to sell certain products, and require online platforms to protect young users. Such rules can reduce the harmful effects of advertising without completely removing it.

Parents also have a key role. They can limit their children's screen time, explain how advertising works and teach them to think critically about what they see. Schools can include **media literacy** in the curriculum, helping children understand persuasion techniques.

In conclusion, advertising aimed at children raises serious concerns because children are easily influenced, and many adverts promote unhealthy products and materialistic values. Strict regulation, combined with education at home and at school, is the best way to **protect children** while allowing responsible advertising to continue.

### Advertising, Consumerism and Buying What We Don't Need

Modern economies depend heavily on consumer spending. Companies spend enormous sums every year on adverts online, on television, in magazines and on billboards, encouraging people to buy more and more products. This culture of constant buying, known as **consumerism**, has brought prosperity but also serious personal and environmental problems.

Consumerism has clear economic benefits. When people buy goods and services, businesses grow, factories produce more and **jobs are created** in manufacturing, retail, transport and advertising. Governments receive more tax income, which they can spend on public services. Competition between companies also encourages **innovation**, leading to better, cheaper and more convenient products. Many people today enjoy a standard of living that previous generations could only dream of, with modern appliances, smartphones and a wide variety of food and clothing.

Advertising also provides useful information. It tells consumers about new products, special offers and services that may improve their lives. It allows small businesses to reach customers and helps to fund media, including newspapers, television and free online services.

However, consumerism encourages people to buy things that they **do not really need**. Advertisers are skilled at creating desires, suggesting that a new phone, car or fashion item will bring happiness, success or social status. Many people replace products that still work perfectly well simply because a newer model is available. Social media influencers add to this pressure by constantly showing new purchases.

There are several reasons why people buy things they do not need. Some want to keep up with friends and neighbours or to show their status. Others shop to relieve stress or boredom. Easy credit, online shopping and constant special offers make spending quick and convenient, while advertising makes people feel that they are always missing something.

The negative effects are significant. Many people **fall into debt** through credit cards and loans, which can cause stress and family problems. Excessive buying also creates huge amounts of **waste**, including clothes worn only a few times, broken electronics and plastic packaging. Producing so many goods uses large amounts of energy, water and raw materials, contributing to pollution and climate change. Moreover, buying more does not necessarily make people happier; many people feel empty or dissatisfied despite owning many possessions.

To reduce these problems, individuals can become more **conscious consumers**, asking themselves whether they really need a product before buying it, and choosing durable, repairable goods. Governments can regulate misleading advertising, encourage recycling and repair, and educate people about financial management. Businesses can also promote sustainable products.

In conclusion, consumerism supports economic growth and offers people many choices, but it also leads to debt, waste and environmental damage. A more balanced approach, in which people **buy less but better**, would benefit both individuals and the planet.

### Fake News and Misinformation

The internet and social media have made it easier than ever to share information. Unfortunately, they have also made it easier to spread **fake news**: false or misleading stories presented as real news. Fake news can be created deliberately to deceive people, attract attention, earn money from advertising or achieve political goals. It has become one of the most serious problems of the digital age.

There are several reasons why fake news spreads so quickly. On social media, anyone can publish information without checking whether it is true. Stories that are shocking, emotional or frightening attract more clicks, likes and shares, and **social media algorithms** often show such content to more people. Many users share stories without reading them fully or checking the source, especially if the story matches what they already believe. Messaging apps allow rumours to spread rapidly through family and friend groups, where people tend to trust what they receive.

The consequences of fake news can be very serious. False rumours have caused panic, for example about food shortages or disease outbreaks, leading people to hoard goods or avoid medical treatment. In some cases, rumours spread on social media have led to violence against innocent people. Health misinformation, such as false claims about vaccines or miracle cures, can put lives at risk.

Fake news also damages **trust in the media** and in institutions. When people are exposed to many false stories, they may begin to doubt all news, including accurate reporting by professional journalists. This makes it harder for societies to agree on basic facts and to make good decisions. During elections, fake news can be used to influence voters and divide communities.

Tackling fake news requires action from several groups. **Social media companies** have a major responsibility. They can use technology and human reviewers to identify and remove false information, label misleading content and reduce the spread of stories from unreliable sources. They should also be transparent about how their algorithms work.

Governments can support independent **fact-checking organisations** and introduce laws against the deliberate spreading of harmful lies. However, they must be careful not to use such laws to silence criticism or limit freedom of speech, which is essential in a democracy.

Perhaps the most important solution is **media literacy**. Schools should teach students how to evaluate sources, check facts and recognise manipulation. Adults can be encouraged, through public campaigns, to pause before sharing and to verify information using trusted news organisations.

In conclusion, fake news spreads quickly because of social media, emotional content and a lack of verification, and it can cause panic, violence and loss of trust. By combining responsible technology companies, careful government action and better media education, societies can **limit the spread of misinformation**.

### News Values: Bad News, Objectivity and Photographs

News plays a vital role in society by informing people about events that affect their lives. However, many people notice that most news is negative, focusing on wars, disasters, accidents and crime. There are also debates about whether news can ever be completely objective, and whether photographs used in the news are reliable.

There are several reasons why bad news dominates. Firstly, negative events are often **urgent and important**. People need to know about natural disasters, disease outbreaks, conflicts and crimes so that they can protect themselves and respond. Secondly, bad news attracts attention. Humans naturally pay more attention to danger, and dramatic or shocking stories attract more viewers, readers and clicks. Because news organisations depend on audiences for advertising income, they have a commercial incentive to focus on such stories.

However, constant negative news can have harmful effects. It can make people **anxious, fearful and pessimistic**, and some people stop following the news altogether. It can also create a distorted picture of the world, making things seem worse than they are, even when health, education and living standards are improving. Many people therefore argue that the news should include a better balance of good and bad stories, such as scientific achievements, acts of kindness and successful community projects.

**Objectivity** is another important issue. Ideally, news should report facts accurately and fairly, without the journalist's personal opinions. In reality, complete objectivity is difficult. Journalists and editors must choose which stories to cover, which facts to include and which words to use, and these choices can reflect their own views or those of the owners. Some news organisations openly support particular political parties. This is why it is important for readers to use several sources and to distinguish between news reports and opinion articles.

The main function of a newspaper or news channel is to **inform the public** accurately. A free press also plays a watchdog role, investigating corruption and holding powerful people accountable. Good journalism helps citizens make informed decisions, especially during elections.

**Photographs** are widely used to support news stories, because they make news more powerful and believable. A single image of a flood or a war can communicate more emotion than many paragraphs. However, photographs are not always reliable. They can be edited digitally, taken out of context, or chosen to present only one side of a story. With modern technology, even video can be faked. Therefore, while photographs are valuable evidence, they should be checked and accompanied by accurate information.

In conclusion, bad news dominates because it is important and attracts attention, but a better balance would give people a more accurate view of the world. News can never be perfectly objective, and even photographs can mislead, so both journalists and readers must be **careful and critical**.

### Media Responsibility and Press Freedom

The media, including newspapers, television, radio and online news platforms, has enormous power to shape public opinion. It decides which issues people talk about and how those issues are presented. With this power comes great responsibility. At the same time, a free media is essential for democracy, which raises questions about how much control governments should have over the press.

A responsible media should report **accurately and fairly**. Journalists should check their facts, use reliable sources, correct mistakes and present different points of view on controversial issues. They should avoid **sensationalism**, which means exaggerating stories to attract attention, and they should respect people's privacy and dignity, especially victims of crime, children and grieving families.

The media also has a responsibility to **inform and educate** the public. It can raise awareness of important social issues, such as health, the environment, poverty and corruption. Investigative journalism can expose wrongdoing by politicians, businesses and other powerful groups, which helps to hold them accountable. In this way, the press is often described as a "watchdog" of democracy.

However, the media does not always meet these standards. Commercial pressure can lead news organisations to focus on celebrity gossip, scandals and dramatic stories rather than serious issues. Some media outlets are owned by wealthy individuals or political groups who use them to promote their own interests. Biased reporting can mislead the public and increase divisions in society. On social media, the line between journalism and personal opinion has become increasingly unclear.

The media also has a special responsibility during emergencies, such as floods, cyclones or disease outbreaks. Accurate and calm reporting can save lives by telling people where to find help and how to stay safe, while exaggerated or false reports can cause panic. Responsible broadcasters work closely with experts and official agencies to share reliable information quickly.

This leads to the question of **government control**. Some people argue that governments should regulate the media more strictly to prevent false information, hate speech and invasions of privacy. Certain rules, such as laws against defamation and incitement to violence, are necessary. However, too much government control is dangerous. In some countries, governments use censorship to silence critics, close independent newspapers and imprison journalists. When the press is not free, citizens cannot learn the truth about their leaders, and corruption can grow.

The best approach is a balance between **freedom and responsibility**. The media should be free from political interference, but journalists should follow strong professional and ethical codes. Independent press councils, rather than governments, can handle complaints and ensure standards. Media owners should be transparent, and audiences should support quality journalism.

Citizens also have a role. By choosing reliable news sources, reading critically and rejecting sensational or false stories, the public can encourage responsible journalism.

In conclusion, the media has a responsibility to report truthfully, fairly and in the public interest. While some regulation is necessary, **press freedom** must be protected, because an independent media is essential for democracy and accountability.

### Celebrities, Influencers and Role Models

In the past, people usually became famous because of their achievements, such as scientific discoveries, artistic talent, sporting success or leadership. Today, many people become famous through television, social media and online videos, sometimes simply because of their appearance, wealth or lifestyle. Celebrities and social media influencers have a huge influence on young people, which raises questions about whether they are good role models.

Many people believe that celebrities today are famous more for their **glamour and wealth** than for their achievements. Reality television stars and influencers often show expensive clothes, cars, holidays and luxurious lifestyles. Young people who follow them may come to believe that money, fame and appearance are the most important things in life. This can encourage materialism and unrealistic expectations, and some young people may feel unhappy because they cannot achieve such lifestyles.

Celebrities can also set **a bad example** through their behaviour. When famous people are involved in drug use, violence, cheating or rude behaviour, young fans may think such behaviour is acceptable or even attractive. Influencers sometimes promote unhealthy diets, dangerous challenges or products without proper information.

However, many celebrities are **positive role models**. Sports stars show that success requires hard work, discipline and teamwork. Musicians and actors can inspire creativity, and many famous people use their influence to support charities, raise awareness of social issues and encourage young people to study or protect the environment. A footballer from a poor background who becomes successful can show young people that determination can overcome difficulties.

Because young children often idolise sports people and entertainers, many believe that celebrities have a **responsibility** to behave well at all times. Their actions are widely reported, and children copy them. Others argue that celebrities are ordinary human beings who did not choose to be role models, and that they should be judged mainly by their professional performance. Parents and teachers, rather than celebrities, should be children's main role models.

In reality, both sides have a point. Celebrities cannot be perfect, but because they benefit from public attention and money from fans and sponsors, they should accept some responsibility to behave decently in public. Sports organisations and companies can also encourage good behaviour through codes of conduct.

Parents and schools play the most important role. They can help young people think critically about celebrity culture, understand that social media images are often edited and unrealistic, and choose role models based on values and achievements rather than fame. Highlighting real-life heroes, such as doctors, scientists, teachers and community leaders, can provide more meaningful examples.

In conclusion, celebrities and influencers can be both positive and negative role models. While some promote glamour and poor behaviour, others inspire hard work and generosity. Young people should be taught to **look beyond fame** and to admire people for their character and achievements.

### Privacy of Famous People

Famous people, such as film stars, singers, sports players and politicians, attract a great deal of public attention. Newspapers, magazines, websites and photographers, known as paparazzi, often follow them closely, reporting on their relationships, families, health and daily activities. Some people believe that famous people should have no right to privacy, while others argue that everyone, however famous, deserves a private life.

Those who believe that celebrities should accept media attention give several reasons. Firstly, many celebrities **actively seek publicity** to build their careers. They use the media to promote their films, music or products and benefit financially from their fame. Therefore, some argue, they cannot complain when the media also reports on their private lives. Secondly, the public is genuinely interested in the lives of famous people, and celebrity news sells newspapers and attracts online readers.

There are also cases where reporting on private matters is in the **public interest**. If a politician is corrupt, lies to the public or behaves in a way that contradicts their public promises, journalists have a duty to expose this. Similarly, if a celebrity who promotes a healthy lifestyle is secretly involved in drug use, reporting this may be justified because it affects how the public sees their message.

However, there are strong arguments for protecting the privacy of famous people. Celebrities are human beings who need personal space, just like everyone else. Constant attention from photographers and journalists can cause **stress, anxiety and depression**. In some tragic cases, intense press pursuit has contributed to serious accidents and mental health crises. Being followed everywhere, having private conversations published or seeing false rumours in the media can make normal life almost impossible.

The families of famous people, particularly their **children**, are also affected. These children did not choose to be famous, yet they may be photographed at school or in the park and have their lives discussed publicly. This can damage their safety and emotional well-being.

Moreover, much celebrity reporting is not in the public interest; it is simply gossip. Stories about who a star is dating or what they wore to the supermarket do not help the public make important decisions. Such reporting is mainly driven by profit.

The best approach is to distinguish between **public interest** and **public curiosity**. The media should be free to report on the professional activities of famous people and on any behaviour that affects the public, such as corruption or hypocrisy. However, their private relationships, health, homes and families should be protected unless there is a genuine public interest. Laws against harassment and the publication of photographs taken in private places can help, as can strong ethical codes for journalists.

In conclusion, famous people inevitably receive more attention than ordinary citizens, and some reporting on their lives is justified. However, they still deserve a basic **right to privacy**, especially regarding their families and personal lives.

### Social Media versus Traditional News

The way people receive news has changed dramatically in recent years. In the past, most people relied on newspapers, radio and television. Today, a growing number of people, especially young people, get their news mainly from social media platforms and online websites. This shift has brought both advantages and serious challenges.

Social media offers several advantages as a news source. It is **fast**: news can be shared within seconds of an event happening, often with photos and videos from people at the scene. It is usually **free** and available on mobile phones anywhere. Social media also allows ordinary people to share information and opinions, giving a voice to groups that traditional media may ignore. During natural disasters or protests, citizens' posts can provide valuable information when journalists are not present.

Online news is also **interactive**. Readers can comment, discuss and share stories, and they can choose the topics they are most interested in. Many traditional newspapers and TV channels now have websites and social media accounts, combining professional journalism with the speed of the internet.

However, social media has serious weaknesses as a source of news. The biggest problem is **reliability**. Anyone can post information, and much of it is not checked. As a result, rumours, fake news and propaganda spread easily. People may find it difficult to tell the difference between professional journalism and personal opinion or deliberate lies.

Another issue is the **echo chamber** effect. Social media algorithms show users content similar to what they have already liked or shared. Over time, people may see only news that confirms their existing views, which can increase division and reduce understanding of different perspectives. Short posts and headlines also encourage quick reading rather than deep understanding of complex issues.

Traditional media, such as newspapers and television news, usually employ professional journalists and editors who check facts and follow ethical standards. They provide more **in-depth analysis**, investigative reporting and balanced coverage. However, traditional media also has weaknesses: it can be slower, may be influenced by owners or advertisers, and many newspapers are struggling financially as readers move online.

Radio is an interesting case. Some people predict that online media will make radio disappear. However, radio remains popular because people can listen while driving, working or cooking, and it is important in rural areas and during emergencies when electricity or internet access is limited. Many radio stations now broadcast online as podcasts, adapting to new technology.

In conclusion, social media has made news faster, cheaper and more interactive, but it has also made it less reliable. The best approach is to **combine sources**, using social media for quick updates while relying on trusted professional news organisations to verify information and provide deeper understanding.

### Television and Children's Behaviour

Television has been a major part of family life for decades, and today children also watch programmes on tablets and smartphones through streaming services and video platforms. Many parents and teachers are concerned about how television affects children's behaviour. Some people believe that the **content** children watch is the main influence, while others think that the **amount of time** spent watching is more important.

Those who focus on content argue that children learn by imitation. Young children often copy the words, actions and attitudes they see on screen. If they watch programmes that show violence, rudeness or dangerous stunts, they may repeat this behaviour at home or at school. Cartoons and films that present violence as funny or heroic may make children less sensitive to the suffering of others. On the other hand, high-quality programmes can teach children positive values, such as kindness, cooperation and respect, as well as knowledge about science, nature and languages.

Advertising is also part of television content. Adverts aimed at children can influence their desires, eating habits and attitudes towards possessions, encouraging them to ask for junk food and expensive toys.

Others argue that the **amount of time** spent watching is the bigger problem, regardless of content. Children who watch television for many hours a day spend less time playing outside, reading, talking with family and making friends. This can affect their physical health, leading to inactivity and weight gain, and their development, including language skills, imagination and concentration. Watching screens late in the evening can also disturb sleep, leaving children tired and irritable the next day.

Excessive screen time may also reduce children's ability to concentrate on slower activities, such as reading a book or listening in class, because television offers constant stimulation. In addition, when children watch television alone in their rooms, parents may not know what they are watching.

In reality, both content and time are important, and they are connected. Even excellent educational programmes can be harmful if children watch them for many hours instead of being active and social. At the same time, a short amount of violent or inappropriate content can have a negative influence.

Parents play the key role in managing television use. They can choose suitable, age-appropriate programmes, set clear limits on daily screen time and keep televisions and devices out of children's bedrooms. Watching programmes together and discussing them helps children understand what they see. Television channels and streaming services can support parents through **age ratings**, parental controls and responsible scheduling.

In conclusion, both what children watch and how long they watch affect their behaviour. Careful choice of content, reasonable time limits and active parental involvement can ensure that television becomes a **positive tool** for learning and entertainment rather than a harmful influence.

### Films, Cinema and Streaming Services

Films have been one of the most popular forms of entertainment for more than a century. Traditionally, people watched new films in cinemas, sharing the experience with friends, families and strangers in a large dark hall. Today, online **streaming services** allow people to watch thousands of films and series at home, on televisions, laptops and phones. Some people believe that cinemas will eventually close because of this trend.

Streaming services have many advantages. They are **convenient**: viewers can watch whenever and wherever they like, pause and replay scenes, and choose from a huge library of content. They are often cheaper than regular cinema visits, especially for families, because a single monthly subscription allows unlimited viewing. Streaming also gives audiences access to films and series from many countries, which can broaden their cultural understanding. For filmmakers, streaming platforms provide new opportunities to reach global audiences without traditional distribution networks.

However, cinemas offer an experience that home viewing cannot fully replace. The **big screen**, powerful sound systems and immersive atmosphere make films, especially action and fantasy films, more exciting and memorable. Going to the cinema is also a **social activity**; friends and families go out together, share reactions and talk about the film afterwards. For many people, it is a special occasion rather than just a way to watch a story. In addition, people watching at home are often distracted by phones, housework and interruptions.

For these reasons, cinemas are unlikely to disappear completely, although they may need to change. Many cinemas now offer luxury seats, 3D and IMAX screens, food and drink services and special events to attract audiences. Major films are often released in cinemas first, which encourages people to visit. However, smaller cinemas, especially in towns with limited audiences, may struggle to survive.

Streaming has also changed the film industry in other ways. Local and independent filmmakers can now reach international audiences, and viewers can discover films in many languages with subtitles. At the same time, the huge choice available online can make it harder for small films to be noticed, and some people worry that watching alone at home reduces the shared cultural experience that cinema once provided.

Another debate concerns **historical films**. Some people believe that films based on real historical events should be completely accurate. Accuracy is important because many people learn about history mainly from films, and inaccurate portrayals can spread false ideas about real people and events. On the other hand, films are works of art and entertainment, not documentaries. Filmmakers often need to simplify events, combine characters or add dialogue to create an engaging story. A reasonable approach is for films to remain faithful to the main facts while clearly stating when parts of the story have been changed or imagined.

In conclusion, streaming services have transformed how people watch films, offering convenience and choice. However, cinemas still provide a unique social and visual experience, so they are likely to **adapt rather than disappear**. Meanwhile, historical films should balance entertainment with respect for the truth.

### Books, E-books and Reading

For centuries, books have been a major source of knowledge and entertainment. Today, however, people have many other options, such as television, the internet, social media, video games and podcasts. At the same time, digital technology has introduced **e-books**, which can be read on tablets, phones and e-readers. Some people believe that books are losing their importance and that e-books will eventually replace paper books.

It is true that many people, especially young people, spend less time reading books than in the past. For quick information, people often search online rather than consult a book, and for entertainment, many prefer videos and games, which require less concentration. Short social media posts have also changed reading habits, making it harder for some people to focus on long texts.

However, books still offer unique benefits. Reading a book requires **deep concentration** and imagination, which helps to develop vocabulary, critical thinking and empathy. Novels allow readers to experience other people's lives and emotions, while non-fiction books provide detailed, well-organised knowledge that is often more reliable than random online information. Many people also read to relax and escape from the stress of daily life. For these reasons, books remain important in education and in personal development.

**E-books** have several advantages. They are often cheaper than paper books, and some are free. A single device can store thousands of books, which is convenient for travellers and students. E-books can be bought and downloaded instantly, and readers can adjust the font size, which helps people with poor eyesight. They also save paper and storage space.

Nevertheless, many people still prefer **paper books**. They enjoy the feel and smell of real pages, find it easier to concentrate without the distractions of notifications, and do not suffer from eye strain caused by screens. Paper books do not need batteries and can be shared, lent or kept as part of a personal library. The continued sales of printed books suggest that paper books are not disappearing.

Reading habits often begin in childhood. Children who are read to by their parents and who have access to books at home and at school usually develop stronger language skills and a lasting love of reading. Encouraging reading for pleasure, through school libraries, book clubs and reading competitions, is therefore one of the best ways to ensure that books remain important for future generations.

Libraries are also adapting. Some people believe libraries should focus on technology and digital resources rather than paper books. Digital resources make information accessible to more people, but libraries also provide a quiet place to study, free access for people who cannot afford books or devices, and community activities. A balance between digital and printed collections is likely to serve the public best.

In conclusion, books face strong competition from digital entertainment, and e-books are becoming more popular. However, reading remains valuable, and paper books and e-books are likely to **coexist**, each meeting different needs and preferences.

### Sponsorship and Marketing

Marketing and advertising are essential tools for businesses. Companies promote their products through television adverts, social media campaigns, billboards and **sponsorship** of sports, music and cultural events. Some people believe that marketing is the key to business success, while others think that a good product will sell without much promotion.

**Sports sponsorship** is a common form of marketing. Companies pay large sums to have their names on players' shirts, stadiums and tournaments. This brings clear benefits to sport. Sponsorship money can fund better facilities, pay players and support young athletes and grassroots clubs. Without sponsors, many tournaments and teams could not exist, and ticket prices would be higher. For companies, sponsorship links their brand with popular events and positive values such as teamwork and success.

However, sports sponsorship also has disadvantages. Some sponsors sell products that are harmful to health, such as fast food, sugary drinks or, in some countries, alcohol and gambling services. When these brands are associated with sport, which is supposed to promote health, young fans may receive confusing messages. There is also a risk that sponsors gain too much influence over sporting decisions, such as match times or player choices, putting profit before the interests of sport and fans.

Similar debates exist about **advertising unhealthy products**. Some people believe that fast food companies should not be allowed to advertise, just as tobacco advertising is banned in many countries, because their products contribute to obesity and related diseases. Others argue that all legal businesses should have the right to advertise, and that adults should be free to make their own choices. A balanced approach might involve banning such adverts during children's programmes and requiring clear nutritional information.

Social media has transformed marketing. Companies now pay influencers to promote their products, often in ways that look like personal recommendations rather than adverts. This can be effective, but it raises concerns about honesty, especially when young followers do not realise that the influencer is being paid. Many countries now require influencers to clearly label sponsored content.

Is marketing really the key to business success? Marketing certainly helps businesses to reach customers, build brand recognition and compete with rivals. Large companies with big marketing budgets can dominate markets, making it difficult for small local businesses to compete, even if their products are good. However, marketing alone cannot guarantee long-term success. If a product is poor quality or overpriced, customers will not buy it again, and negative reviews spread quickly online. **Product quality**, good customer service, fair prices and innovation are equally important.

Even an excellent product needs some promotion, because customers cannot buy something they do not know exists. However, word of mouth and online reviews can be powerful forms of free advertising for products that genuinely meet customers' needs.

In conclusion, marketing and sponsorship bring important benefits, including funding for sport and greater visibility for businesses. However, they must be used responsibly, especially for unhealthy products. Ultimately, business success depends on combining effective marketing with **genuinely good products and services**.

#### 1. Long simple sentence (rich noun phrase)

**Formula:** [long noun phrase] + verb + object
*একটাই clause, কিন্তু subject-টা বড় ও তথ্যপূর্ণ।*

- The rapid spread of false stories on social media platforms **has damaged** public trust in the news.
- Colourful television adverts for sugary snacks and soft drinks **encourage** unhealthy eating among children.

#### 2. Compound with *and / but / so / yet*

- Advertising supports the economy, **but** it also encourages waste.
- Children cannot recognise persuasion, **so** they are easily influenced by adverts.

#### 3. Compound with semicolon + linker

- Social media is fast**; however,** it is often unreliable.
- Celebrities are idolised by children**; therefore,** they should behave responsibly.

#### 4. Complex — concession (*Although / Even though / While*)

- **Although** streaming is convenient, cinemas offer a unique shared experience.
- **Even though** advertising funds free content, it can manipulate consumers.

#### 5. Complex — reason (*Because / Since / As*)

- **Since** shocking stories attract more viewers, the news often focuses on disasters.
- **Because** fake news spreads quickly, it can cause panic before it is corrected.

#### 6. Complex — condition (*If / Unless / Provided that*)

- **If** people checked sources before sharing, fake news **would** spread more slowly.
- **Unless** advertising to children is regulated, childhood obesity **will** continue to rise.

#### 7. Relative clause (*which / who / where / whose*)

- Influencers **who** promote unhealthy diets can harm young followers.
- Many children watch adverts for fast food, **which** contributes to obesity.

#### 8. Compound-complex

**Formula:** Although/Because/If + clause, Clause A, and/but Clause B

- **Although** celebrities did not choose to be role models, children copy them, **and** this gives them a responsibility.
- **If** journalists check their facts, readers trust them, **and** the media becomes more powerful.

#### 9. Participle phrase

- **Exposed** to constant advertising, children want more and more products.
- **Shared** thousands of times, a false rumour can quickly become "truth" in people's minds.

#### 10. *Not only … but also …*

- Fake news **not only** misleads people **but also** damages trust in real journalism.
- Sponsorship **not only** funds sport **but also** promotes the sponsor's brand.

#### 11. *The more …, the more …*

- **The more** adverts children see, **the more** they ask their parents to buy.
- **The more** shocking a story is, **the faster** it spreads online.

#### 12. Cleft sentence (emphasis)

- **It is** the speed of social media, not the internet itself, **that** makes fake news so dangerous.
- **What** children need **is** guidance on how to understand advertising.

#### 13. Impersonal passive

- **It is often argued that** the media focuses too much on bad news.
- **It is widely believed that** advertising encourages people to buy unnecessary products.

#### 14. Contrasting views

- **While some people believe that** celebrities deserve privacy, **others argue that** fame comes with public attention.
- **While** traditional newspapers check facts, **social media posts** often do not.

#### 15. Result / consequence

- Many news outlets depend on advertising, **which means that** they compete for clicks.
- Adverts make children want junk food. **As a result,** family arguments become common.

### Essay 1 — Agree / Disagree

<div class="question">One of the prime times for advertising on TV is when children get back from school. Some people think that advertisements aimed at children should not be allowed. What is your opinion?</div>

#### Introduction (35–50 words)

অনেক কোম্পানি বিকেলে, যখন শিশুরা স্কুল থেকে বাড়ি ফেরে, তখন টেলিভিশনে বিজ্ঞাপন দেখায়, এবং কেউ কেউ মনে করেন যে শিশুদের লক্ষ্য করে তৈরি বিজ্ঞাপন নিষিদ্ধ করা উচিত। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> আমি এই মতের সাথে সম্পূর্ণ একমত, কারণ শিশুরা সহজেই প্রভাবিত হয় এবং অনেক বিজ্ঞাপন অস্বাস্থ্যকর খাবারের প্রচার করে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — Idea 1 (100–120 words)

এই ধারণার সাথে আমার একমত হওয়ার প্রধান কারণ হলো, ছোট শিশুরা সহজেই প্রভাবিত (easily influenced) হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> এর কারণ হলো, শিশুরা প্রায়ই বোঝে না যে বিজ্ঞাপনের উদ্দেশ্য হলো তাদের কিছু কিনতে রাজি করানো। <span class="tag">Complex</span> তারা screen-এ যা দেখে তা-ই বিশ্বাস করে; ফলে বিজ্ঞাপনে দেখানো প্রতিটি খেলনা, খাবার বা game তারা চাইতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, চিনিযুক্ত cereal-এর একটি কার্টুন বিজ্ঞাপন দেখা সাত বছরের একটি শিশু পরদিন বাবা-মায়ের কাছে সেটি কেনার জন্য বায়না ধরতে পারে, এবং এতে পরিবারে ঝগড়া ও মানসিক চাপ তৈরি হতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> তাই এত ছোট দর্শকদের লক্ষ্য করে কোম্পানিগুলোর বিজ্ঞাপন দেওয়া অন্যায্য। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — Idea 2 (100–120 words)

আমার একমত হওয়ার আরেকটি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ কারণ হলো, অনেক বিজ্ঞাপন অস্বাস্থ্যকর খাবারের প্রচার করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> Fast food, মিষ্টি ও কোমল পানীয়ের বিজ্ঞাপন যখন কার্টুন চরিত্র ও বিনামূল্যের খেলনা নিয়ে আসে, তখন এই পণ্যগুলো ছোট দর্শকদের কাছে আকর্ষণীয় ও মজার মনে হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যেমন, যে শিশু প্রতি বিকেলে burger-এর বিজ্ঞাপন দেখে, সে সপ্তাহে কয়েকবার fast food চাইতে পারে, যা অস্বাস্থ্যকর খাদ্যাভ্যাস এবং পরবর্তী জীবনে স্থূলতার (obesity) দিকে নিয়ে যেতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে নিষেধাজ্ঞা কোম্পানি ও টিভি চ্যানেলের ক্ষতি করবে, শিশুদের স্বাস্থ্য রক্ষা করা বেশি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ, আর ব্যবসাগুলো তখনও প্রাপ্তবয়স্কদের জন্য বিজ্ঞাপন দিতে পারবে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> ফলে সরকারের উচিত কঠোর নিয়ম চালু করা, যা ছোট দর্শকদের এ ধরনের বিজ্ঞাপন থেকে রক্ষা করবে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Conclusion (35–55 words)

পরিশেষে, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে শিশুদের জন্য টেলিভিশন বিজ্ঞাপনের অনুমতি দেওয়া উচিত নয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ছোট দর্শকেরা এই বার্তাগুলো ঠিকভাবে বিচার করতে পারে না, এবং এগুলো নিষিদ্ধ করলে শিশুরা অস্বাস্থ্যকর খাবার খাওয়ার চাপ থেকেও রক্ষা পাবে। <span class="tag">Compound</span>

### Essay 2 — Positive and Negative Effects

<div class="question">Companies spend millions each year on advertising online, in magazines and on billboards. These adverts can encourage people to buy goods that they do not really need. What are the positive and negative effects of consumerism?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · MED-10 · Common topic ★ Too much advertising*

**Essay type:** Advantages–Disadvantages, Type 1 (positive and negative effects, no opinion asked) · **Position:** No opinion is asked for, so both sides are discussed in balance.<br>
**অবস্থান:** প্রশ্নে শুধু ইতিবাচক ও নেতিবাচক প্রভাব চাওয়া হয়েছে, মতামত নয়। তাই মতামত বা plan sentence নেই। Although ও While বাক্যে আগে অসুবিধা, comma-র পরে সুবিধা।<br>
**কাঠামো:** IELTS_Advantages_Disadvantages_Essay_Guide.md অনুযায়ী — Type 1 (মতামত চাওয়া হয়নি) — Although-introduction, প্রতিটি body paragraph-এ ঠিক 7টি বাক্য, While-conclusion। মতামত বা "This essay will…" বাক্য নেই। মোট 310–340 শব্দ।

**পরিকল্পনা (Plan)**

- **Introduction (30–40):** paraphrase → Although + নেতিবাচক (ঋণ, পরিবেশের ক্ষতি), + ইতিবাচক (প্রবৃদ্ধি, উদ্ভাবন)। মতামত নেই।
- **Body Paragraph 1 (120–135) — ইতিবাচক, 7টি বাক্য:** অর্থনৈতিক প্রবৃদ্ধি → বেশি উৎপাদন, বেশি কর্মী → উদাহরণ: বাংলাদেশের পোশাকশিল্প → কর্মসংস্থান ও উন্নত পণ্য → প্রতিযোগিতা → Overall…
- **Body Paragraph 2 (120–135) — নেতিবাচক, 7টি বাক্য:** নেই এমন টাকা খরচ → সহজ ঋণে দেনা → উদাহরণ: প্রতি বছর ঋণে ফোন কেনা তরুণ → বর্জ্য → ফেলে দেওয়া সস্তা পোশাক → Overall, these…
- **Conclusion (25–35):** নতুন paraphrase → While + নেতিবাচক, + ইতিবাচক। মতামত নেই।

#### Introduction (30–40 words)

কোম্পানিগুলো বিজ্ঞাপনে বিপুল অর্থ ব্যয় করে, যা প্রায়ই মানুষকে এমন জিনিস কিনতে প্ররোচিত করে, যা তাদের আসলে দরকার নেই। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও ভোগবাদ (consumerism) ঋণ ও পরিবেশের ক্ষতির কারণ হতে পারে, এটি অর্থনৈতিক প্রবৃদ্ধিতে সহায়তা করে এবং উদ্ভাবনকে উৎসাহিত করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — ইতিবাচক প্রভাব (120–135 words)

এতে কোনো সন্দেহ নেই যে ভোগবাদ আধুনিক অর্থনীতির জন্য উল্লেখযোগ্য সুবিধা বয়ে আনে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> প্রথমত, সবচেয়ে বড় সুবিধা হলো অর্থনৈতিক প্রবৃদ্ধিতে (economic growth) এর অবদান। <span class="tag">Simple</span> এর কারণ হলো, মানুষ যখন বেশি পণ্য কেনে, তখন কোম্পানিগুলো বেশি উৎপাদন করে এবং কারখানা, দোকান ও পরিবহনে আরও বেশি কর্মী নিয়োগ দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, পোশাকের জোরালো বৈশ্বিক চাহিদার কারণে বাংলাদেশের পোশাকশিল্পে লাখ লাখ শ্রমিক কাজ করেন, এবং তাদের মজুরিতে পুরো পরিবার চলে। <span class="tag">Compound · Passive</span> এছাড়া, ভোক্তাদের খরচ শুধু কর্মসংস্থানই তৈরি করে না, কোম্পানিগুলোকে পণ্যের মান উন্নত করতেও উৎসাহিত করে। <span class="tag">Simple</span> ব্র্যান্ডগুলোর মধ্যে তীব্র প্রতিযোগিতার ফলে প্রতিষ্ঠানগুলো গ্রাহকদের জন্য আরও ভালো, সস্তা ও সুবিধাজনক পণ্য তৈরি করে। <span class="tag">Simple</span> সব মিলিয়ে, ভোগবাদ কর্মসংস্থান তৈরি করতে, জীবনযাত্রার মান বাড়াতে এবং মানুষের প্রতিদিন ব্যবহৃত পণ্যগুলো উন্নত করতে সাহায্য করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — নেতিবাচক প্রভাব (120–135 words)

অন্যদিকে, এটি অস্বীকার করা যায় না যে ভোগবাদের কিছু অসুবিধাও আছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> প্রথমত, অনেক মানুষ এমন টাকা খরচ করে, যা তাদের কাছে নেই। <span class="tag">Complex</span> সহজ ঋণ কেনাকাটাকে দ্রুত ও সহজ করে দেয়; ফলে অনেকে credit card ও ঋণের মাধ্যমে দেনায় জড়িয়ে পড়ে (fall into debt), যা মানসিক চাপ ও পারিবারিক সমস্যা তৈরি করতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> যেমন, যে তরুণ কর্মী প্রতি বছর ঋণে সর্বশেষ মডেলের ফোন কেনেন, তাকে হয়তো মাসের পর মাস সেই ঋণ শোধ করতে হয়, যা সঞ্চয়ের জন্য খুব কম টাকা রাখে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> দ্বিতীয়ত, অতিরিক্ত কেনাকাটা পরিবেশের জন্য একটি গুরুতর হুমকি হতে পারে, কারণ এটি বিপুল পরিমাণ বর্জ্য তৈরি করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> সস্তা পোশাক যদি মাত্র কয়েকবার পরার পরই ফেলে দেওয়া হয়, তাহলে ময়লার ভাগাড় (landfills) বড় হতে থাকে, এবং ফলস্বরূপ মাটি ও পানি আরও দূষিত হয়। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> সব মিলিয়ে, এই আর্থিক ও পরিবেশগত সমস্যাগুলো ব্যক্তি এবং ভবিষ্যৎ প্রজন্ম — উভয়েরই ক্ষতি করতে পারে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Conclusion (25–35 words)

পরিশেষে, সারাক্ষণ কেনাকাটার সংস্কৃতি এখন অনেক দেশেই সাধারণ। <span class="tag">Simple</span> যদিও (While) ভোগবাদ ব্যক্তিগত ঋণ ও ক্রমবর্ধমান বর্জ্যের দিকে নিয়ে যেতে পারে, এটি অর্থনৈতিক প্রবৃদ্ধি ও উন্নত পণ্যের চালিকাশক্তি। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Introduction (30–40 words)

বেশিরভাগ সংবাদ যুদ্ধ, দুর্ঘটনা ও অপরাধের মতো নেতিবাচক ঘটনার ওপর জোর দেয়। <span class="tag">Simple</span> এর মূল কারণ হলো খারাপ খবর মনোযোগ আকর্ষণ করে এবং টাকা আয় করে, এবং আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে সংবাদে ভালো ও খারাপ খবরের ভারসাম্য থাকা উচিত। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — Q1: List block (90–120 words)

এর একটি প্রধান কারণ হলো, খারাপ খবর মানুষের মনোযোগ আকর্ষণ করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> মানুষ স্বাভাবিকভাবেই বিপদের দিকে বেশি মনোযোগ দেয়, এবং নাটকীয় বা চমকপ্রদ খবর আরও বেশি দর্শক, পাঠক ও click টানে। <span class="tag">Compound</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, একটি সফল সামাজিক উদ্যোগের চেয়ে একটি ভয়াবহ সড়ক দুর্ঘটনার খবর প্রথম পাতায় (front page) আসার সম্ভাবনা অনেক বেশি, এবং এতে পত্রিকা বেশি বিক্রি হয়। <span class="tag">Compound</span> আরেকটি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ কারণ হলো বাণিজ্যিক চাপ (commercial pressure)। <span class="tag">Simple</span> সংবাদ প্রতিষ্ঠানগুলো যেহেতু বিজ্ঞাপনের আয়ের জন্য দর্শকের ওপর নির্ভর করে, তাই এ ধরনের খবরে জোর দেওয়ার বাণিজ্যিক তাগিদ তাদের থাকে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে সবচেয়ে নাটকীয় ঘটনাগুলোই সাধারণত সংবাদের শীর্ষে থাকে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — Q2: Opinion block (90–120 words)

সংবাদে ভারসাম্য থাকা উচিত কি না — এ প্রশ্নে আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে থাকা উচিত, কারণ সারাক্ষণ খারাপ খবর মানুষকে পৃথিবী সম্পর্কে একটি ভুল চিত্র (a false picture) দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> দর্শকেরা যখন শুধু দুর্যোগ ও অপরাধ দেখে, তখন তারা ভাবতে পারে যে জীবন আরও খারাপ হচ্ছে, যদিও অনেক জায়গায় স্বাস্থ্য ও শিক্ষার উন্নতি হচ্ছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যেমন, যে মা শুধু অপরাধের খবর দেখেন, তিনি সন্তানদের বাইরে খেলতে দিতে অতিরিক্ত ভয় পেতে পারেন, যা তাদের স্বাস্থ্য ও আত্মবিশ্বাসের ক্ষতি করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে গুরুতর সমস্যাকে সবসময় অগ্রাধিকার দিতে হবে, সেগুলোর পাশাপাশি ইতিবাচক খবরও প্রচার করা যায়। <span class="tag">Complex · Passive</span> তাই ভালো ও খারাপ খবরের ন্যায্য মিশ্রণ জনগণের আরও ভালো উপকারে আসবে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Conclusion (30–40 words)

পরিশেষে, খারাপ খবর প্রাধান্য পায়, কারণ এটি দর্শক টানে এবং বিজ্ঞাপনের টাকা আনে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে সংবাদে ভালো ও খারাপ খবর মিলিয়ে দেওয়া উচিত, কারণ এতে মানুষ পৃথিবী সম্পর্কে আরও সঠিক ধারণা পায়। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

</div>

| 8 | Did I follow my **essay-type guide** (Part 2G): its structure, its word plan (about **280 words**) and no partial opinion, in 40 minutes? | |

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Common mistakes in media essays (এগুলো এড়িয়ে চলো):**

- ❌ *The news are bad.* → ✅ **The news is** bad. ("news" is uncountable and singular)
- ❌ *a news* → ✅ **a piece of news** / **a news story**
- ❌ *advertisement* used as uncountable → ✅ **advertising** (the industry, uncountable) vs **an advertisement / advert** (one item)
- ❌ *Social medias* → ✅ **social media** (no plural "s")
- ❌ *Media is showing violence every time.* → ✅ *The media often shows violent content.*
- ❌ *Celebrities' private life* → ✅ celebrities' private **lives**
- **"advertising" প্রতিশব্দ:** marketing, promotion, commercials, adverts, publicity, advertising campaigns, brand promotion

</div>

### Homework 1 — Problem–Solution

<div class="question">Fake news is spreading rapidly through social media and messaging apps. What problems does this cause, and what measures can be taken to solve them?</div>

*Source: Practice question · ★ Fake news (no IELTS Liz question on this exact topic)*

**Essay type:** Problem and Solution · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 2 causes/problems 90–120 · BP2 2 solutions 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** Fake News & Misinformation; Social Media vs Traditional News; Media Responsibility

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- Problem + Solution: BP1-এ 2টি সমস্যা (আতঙ্ক ও সহিংসতা; স্বাস্থ্যঝুঁকি) — উদাহরণ: কোনো এলাকা নিয়ে গুজব বা ভুয়া চিকিৎসা পরামর্শ।
- BP2 (Mirror rule): প্রতিটি সমাধান একটি সমস্যার — সোশ্যাল মিডিয়া কোম্পানি (ভুয়া পোস্ট দ্রুত সরানো), স্কুল (media literacy); প্রতিটিতে WHO + WHAT + HOW।
- Introduction: This can lead to P1 and P2, but S1 and S2 can reduce these effects.
- মতামত নয়; উপসংহারে কোনো সমাধানকে "সবচেয়ে গুরুত্বপূর্ণ" বলবে না, দুটোই নতুন শব্দে।

</div>

**Try to use:** fake news / misinformation · go viral · social media algorithms · cause panic · damage trust in the media · fact-checking organisations · media literacy · verify information

**Sentence target:** use *If people…, …would…* once and *not only… but also…* once.

</div>

### Homework 2 — Agree / Disagree

<div class="question">In the last few decades, there have been more and more cases of famous people being hounded by the press. Some people think that famous people in the media should have no right to privacy. To what extent do you agree?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · MED-06 · ★ media responsibility*

**Essay type:** Agree / Disagree — one clear side · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 270–345 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 35–50 · BP1 Idea 1 100–120 · BP2 Idea 2 100–120 · Conclusion 35–55<br>
**Use ideas from:** Privacy of Famous People; Media Responsibility; Celebrities & Role Models

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- "no right to privacy" একটি চরম দাবি, তাই সম্পূর্ণ দ্বিমত নেওয়া সহজ; আংশিক উত্তর নয়।
- Idea 1: তারকারাও মানুষ — মানসিক চাপ, সন্তান ও পরিবারের নিরাপত্তা। Idea 2: বেশিরভাগ খবর শুধু gossip, জনস্বার্থে নয়। প্রতিটির একটি উদাহরণ।
- জনস্বার্থের যুক্তি (দুর্নীতি বা ভণ্ডামি প্রকাশ) BP2-এর Response বাক্যে Although অংশে রাখো।
- উপসংহার: In conclusion, I firmly believe that… + দুটি idea নতুন শব্দে। নতুন কোনো সুপারিশ বা idea নয়।

</div>

**Try to use:** be hounded by the press · paparazzi · an invasion of privacy · public figures · in the public interest · celebrity gossip · mental health · strict ethical codes

**Sentence target:** use one cleft sentence (*It is… that…*) and *Although…, …* twice.

</div>

### Homework 3 — Discussion

<div class="question">More and more newspapers and news channels are using photographs to support their news articles and stories. Some people think that photographs are not a reliable source of news, while others consider photographs to be irrefutable. Discuss both sides and give your opinion.</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · MED-04*

**Essay type:** Discussion (both views + opinion) · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–299 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 35–45 · BP1 (the other view) 90–120 · BP2 (your view) 90–120 · Conclusion 35–45<br>
**Use ideas from:** News Values: Bad News, Objectivity & Photographs; Fake News

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- "irrefutable" মানে যা অস্বীকার করা যায় না। ছবি কেন শক্তিশালী প্রমাণ মনে হয় (চোখে দেখা, আবেগ, ঘটনাস্থলের সত্য)?
- ছবি কেন অবিশ্বস্ত হতে পারে (এডিটিং, প্রসঙ্গের বাইরে ব্যবহার, শুধু এক দিক দেখানো, ভুয়া ভিডিও)? একটি উদাহরণ ভাবো।
- তোমার মত: একটিই পক্ষ বেছে নাও, কখনো আংশিক নয়। সেটি সবসময় BP2-তে রাখো।
- উপসংহারে নতুন কোনো idea বা সুপারিশ নয়: প্রশ্ন নতুন শব্দে, দুই মতের মূল কথা, আর একই মত।

</div>

**Try to use:** reliable source · edited digitally · taken out of context · powerful evidence · present only one side · verify · objective reporting · critical readers

**Sentence target:** use *While some people argue that…, others believe that…* in the introduction and one relative clause with *which*.

</div>

### Homework 4 — Double Question

<div class="question">Most people think that the truth should be objective rather than subjective when it comes to the news. Do you think all news is true? What is the function of a newspaper?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · MED-03 · ★ fake news / media responsibility*

**Essay type:** Double Question — Opinion + List · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 = Q1 (Opinion block) 90–120 · BP2 = Q2 (List block) 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** News Values; Media Responsibility & Press Freedom; Fake News

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- Q1 "Do you think all news is true?" — Opinion block: না, কারণ প্রতিটি খবর মানুষের বাছাইয়ে তৈরি। উদাহরণ: একই নির্বাচনের ফল দুটি পত্রিকায় দুইভাবে।
- Opinion block-এর Response: Although some people argue that প্রশিক্ষিত সাংবাদিক শুধু তথ্যই দেন, [তোমার পক্ষ]।
- Q2 List block: সংবাদপত্রের 2টি কাজ (জনগণকে তথ্য দেওয়া; ক্ষমতাবানদের জবাবদিহি, watchdog); Body 2-তে "I" নয়।
- Introduction: paraphrase + [উত্তর 1], and [উত্তর 2]। Conclusion-এ দুটি উত্তর নতুন শব্দে, নতুন idea নয়।

</div>

**Try to use:** objective / subjective · biased reporting · editors and owners · inform the public · hold the powerful accountable · a watchdog of democracy · investigative journalism · use several sources

**Sentence target:** use *The more…, the more…* once and *Unless…, …* once.

</div>
