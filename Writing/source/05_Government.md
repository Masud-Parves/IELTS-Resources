## Part 1 — Idea Generation Flow {: .part}

<div class="flow">Government → Role of Government (Laws, Lifestyle &amp; Freedom) → Education vs Defence → Public Transport vs Roads → Arts vs Science Funding → Healthcare &amp; Public Services → Space Exploration → Sports Facilities &amp; Big Events → Welfare &amp; Poverty → Foreign Aid → Taxation → Rural Development → Museums &amp; Heritage</div>

When you see **any** government or public-spending question, run through this chain from left to right and ask: *"Does this question touch this area?"* Then **stop at the 2 links that fit the question best**. Those 2 links become your two body paragraphs.

**Memory trick — almost every spending question is a choice between A and B. Judge it with the N-L-F test:**

| Test | Question to ask yourself | Example (space vs poverty) |
|---|---|---|
| **N — Need** | How many people benefit, and how urgent is the need? | poverty affects millions now; space is less urgent |
| **L — Long-term return** | What benefits will this bring in 10–20 years? | satellites, weather warnings, new technology |
| **F — Fairness** | Who pays, who benefits, is anyone left behind? | taxpayers pay; poor countries may not benefit |

Use N for one body paragraph and L or F for the other. In the conclusion, a "balanced budget" answer (fund both, but set clear priorities) is usually the safest and most mature position.

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Example:** *"Governments should spend money on public transport rather than building new roads."*

N → public transport serves more people (students, workers, the poor) ✔ — L → less pollution and traffic in the future ✔ — F → poor people without cars benefit ✔.
Chain links: **Public Transport vs Roads** + **Rural Development** (roads still needed in villages).

</div>

## Part 2 — Exam-Hall Brainstorming System {: .part}

### 2A. The 5-Minute Plan

| Minute | What to do | What you write on the question paper |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | **Decode the question.** Underline the topic words and the instruction words. Find A and B (what is being compared). | e.g. *arts / wasting money / elsewhere → Agree / Disagree guide; A = arts, B = other services* |
| 1–3 | **N-L-F grid** for A and for B. | 4–6 one-line notes |
| 3–4 | **Choose the 2 strongest ideas.** Add one reason and one example to each. | Idea 1 + why + example; Idea 2 + why + example |
| 4–5 | **Decide your position (one clear side, only if the question asks) and use the guide's word plan.** | Thesis / BP1 / BP2 / Conclusion in 4 lines |

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**পরীক্ষার হলে মনে রাখো:** "Government should spend more on A **than** B" ধরনের প্রশ্নে কখনো লিখবে না যে B সম্পূর্ণ বাদ দেওয়া উচিত। পরীক্ষক পরিণত (balanced) চিন্তা পছন্দ করেন। সবচেয়ে ভালো অবস্থান: **"A-কে অগ্রাধিকার দেওয়া উচিত, তবে B-তেও যুক্তিসংগত বাজেট রাখতে হবে।"**

</div>

### 2B. WHO is affected?

- **Taxpayers** — they pay for every public service and want value for money
- **Poor and vulnerable people** — depend most on free schools, hospitals, welfare and public transport
- **Students and young people** — education budget, sports facilities, job opportunities
- **Patients and elderly people** — healthcare spending, pensions, care
- **Artists, musicians and performers** — arts funding, museums, festivals
- **Scientists and researchers** — science and space budgets
- **Rural communities** — roads, electricity, internet, schools, clinics
- **Businesses** — infrastructure, taxes, a skilled workforce
- **The armed forces and citizens' safety** — defence budget
- **Future generations** — long-term investments, debt, heritage

### 2E. How One Idea Becomes a Full Paragraph (PEEL)

**P**oint → **E**xplain (why? how?) → **E**xample → **L**ink / Result

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### বাংলায় চিন্তা (Bangla thinking)

**Point:** শিক্ষায় বিনিয়োগ একটি দেশের দীর্ঘমেয়াদি উন্নয়নের ভিত্তি।
**Explain:** কারণ শিক্ষিত ও দক্ষ জনশক্তি বেশি উৎপাদনশীল হয় এবং নতুন শিল্প আকর্ষণ করে।
**Example:** যেমন, যে দেশ প্রকৌশল ও প্রযুক্তি শিক্ষায় বিনিয়োগ করে, সেখানে বিদেশি প্রতিষ্ঠানগুলো কারখানা ও অফিস খুলতে আগ্রহী হয়।
**Link/Result:** ফলে নতুন চাকরি তৈরি হয় এবং দেশের অর্থনীতি শক্তিশালী হয়।

</div>

**The same idea in English (one paragraph):**

> **Point:** Investment in education is the foundation of a country's long-term development.
> **Explain:** This is because an educated and skilled workforce is more productive and attracts new industries.
> **Example:** For example, when a country invests in engineering and technology education, foreign companies become more interested in opening factories and offices there.
> **Link:** As a result, new jobs are created and the national economy becomes stronger.

Count it: about **60 words** from one idea. Add a second explanation (for example, educated citizens are healthier and less likely to commit crime) and you reach the **100–120 words** that one body paragraph needs.

### 2F. Worked Brainstorm Demo

<div class="question">More developing countries are given aid from international organisations to help them in their development plans. Some people argue that financial aid is important, but others suggest that practical aid and advice are more important. Discuss both views and give your opinion.</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · SOC-04 (also in the Part 7 Question Bank)*

**My 5-minute notes:**

```
Type: Discussion (both views + opinion) → Discuss Both Views guide
Other view (BP1) = financial aid    My view (BP2) = practical aid & advice
N-L-F:  A → Need: fast help in disasters, big projects (bridges, hospitals)
        B → Long-term: skills stay in the country ✔ (my reason)
        F → money can be stolen; training reaches local people directly

Intro (35–45w): While some people argue that money is the most useful aid,
                others believe that practical help and advice matter more.
                I completely support the latter view because skills last longer.
BP1 (90–120w) OTHER VIEW, no "I" – P E E X L:
   money funds large projects and emergencies quickly
   ex: money after a cyclone rebuilds bridges and hospitals
   L: For this reason, many people believe that financial aid matters most
BP2 (90–120w) MY VIEW – P E X R L:
   practical aid builds skills that remain after the aid ends
   ex: foreign experts train local engineers to maintain a water system
   R: Although some argue large projects need money first, money may be
      stolen by officials, while skills cannot be stolen
   L: Consequently, practical aid is more important
Conclusion (35–45w): In conclusion, people hold different views on whether …
                     While …, I firmly believe that … because …
```

### 2G. Essay-Type Paragraph Plans (your five essay guides)

| Essay type (guide) | Introduction | Body Paragraph 1 | Body Paragraph 2 | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Agree / Disagree** (*to what extent? / do you agree?*) | **35–50:** paraphrase + *I completely agree/disagree with this view because* Idea 1 and Idea 2. Never "partly". | **100–120:** Idea 1 — P E E X L | **100–120:** Idea 2 — P E X R L (*Although some people argue that …, [my side]*) | **35–55:** *In conclusion, I firmly believe that …* + both ideas in new words |
| **Discuss Both Views** (*some think X, others think Y*) | **35–45:** *While some people argue that [other view], others believe that [my view]. I completely support the latter view because …* | **90–120:** the view you do **not** support, no "I" — P E E X L | **90–120:** **your view** — P E X R L | **35–45:** *In conclusion, people hold different views on whether … While …, I firmly believe that …* |
| **Advantages – Disadvantages** | **40–50:** paraphrase + *Although* [side you do not support], [side you support] + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **30–40**, 2 sentences) | **120–135:** advantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **120–135:** disadvantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **35–45:** *While …* + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **25–35**) |
| **Cause/Problem + Solution** | **30–40:** paraphrase + *This is mainly because C1 and C2, but S1 and S2 can tackle this problem.* No opinion. | **90–120:** 2 causes or problems — P E X P E L | **90–120:** 2 solutions, each fixing one cause (WHO + WHAT + HOW) | **30–40:** causes + solutions in new words |
| **Double Question** (*two questions*) | **30–40:** paraphrase + *[Answer 1], and [answer 2].* | **90–120:** answer to Q1 — List block (P E X P E L) or Opinion block (P E X R L) | **90–120:** answer to Q2 — List or Opinion block | **30–40:** *In conclusion,* answer 1 + answer 2 in new words |

*Full rules: `Writing/IELTS_*_Essay_Guide.md`. Questions asking only for solutions or only for problems use the Cause/Problem + Solution guide, Section 14: one idea in each body paragraph (P E E X L), no opinion.*

## Part 3 — Category-Wise Free Writing {: .part}

Each passage below is an **idea store**. Read it slowly, notice how each idea is explained and supported with an example, and pay attention to the **bold collocations**. After each passage, try to say 4–5 of its ideas aloud in your own words.

### The Role of Government: Laws, Lifestyle and Individual Freedom

One of the oldest debates in politics is how much power a government should have over people's lives. Some believe that governments should make decisions about citizens' lifestyles, such as what they eat, whether they smoke or how they drive, in order to protect public health and safety. Others argue that adults should be free to make their own choices, even if those choices are not always wise. Closely related is the question of whether real change in society comes mainly from governments or from individuals.

There are strong arguments for government action. Some problems are too large for individuals to solve alone. Only governments can **pass laws**, collect taxes and build national systems such as hospitals, schools, roads and electricity networks. Laws can also protect people from harm. Seat-belt laws, speed limits, bans on smoking in public places and food safety rules have saved many lives. In these cases, a small limit on personal freedom brings a large benefit to society. Moreover, unhealthy choices by individuals can affect others; for example, smoking harms people nearby, and treating lifestyle-related illnesses is expensive for public healthcare systems.

Governments are also responsible for protecting the weak. Children cannot make fully informed decisions, so governments set rules about school attendance, child labour and advertising to children. Similarly, laws protect workers, consumers and minorities from unfair treatment.

However, too much government control can be harmful. Adults should have the **right to make their own decisions** about their private lives, as long as they do not harm others. If governments decide what people eat, wear or do in their free time, citizens may feel that their freedom is being taken away, and they may lose trust in their leaders. In some countries, governments have used "public interest" as an excuse to control people's lives and silence criticism.

Individuals can also have a powerful influence on society. History shows that many important changes began with ordinary people: campaigns for civil rights, women's right to vote, environmental protection and independence movements all started with individuals who refused to accept injustice. Today, social media allows a single person to **raise awareness** of a problem and mobilise thousands of supporters. Entrepreneurs, teachers, doctors and volunteers also change society through their daily work.

In practice, the most effective changes happen when governments and individuals work together. Governments can create laws and provide resources, but laws work best when citizens understand and support them. For example, recycling laws are only effective if people actually separate their waste. Similarly, public campaigns often begin with citizens and later become government policy.

In conclusion, governments have an important duty to protect public health and safety, and some lifestyle rules are justified. However, they should respect individual freedom and avoid unnecessary control. Meaningful social change usually depends on **cooperation between governments and citizens**, each playing their own part.

### Education versus Defence

Every government must divide its limited budget between many needs. Two of the largest areas of spending in many countries are education and defence. Some people believe that governments should spend more on education than on the military, while others argue that a strong defence is essential to protect a nation and its citizens. This debate reflects a deeper question about what makes a country truly secure and successful.

Supporters of greater education spending argue that education is the **foundation of long-term development**. A well-educated population is more productive, more innovative and better able to compete in the global economy. Educated citizens tend to earn higher incomes, pay more taxes, live healthier lives and are less likely to commit crimes. Investment in schools and universities also reduces poverty and inequality, because it gives children from poor families the chance to improve their lives. From this point of view, money spent on education **pays for itself** many times over.

In addition, education can contribute to peace and security in its own way. When young people have good schools and job opportunities, they are less likely to be drawn into crime, violence or extremist groups. Education also promotes understanding between different communities, which reduces conflict.

On the other hand, those who support defence spending argue that a government's **first responsibility** is to protect its citizens. A country needs armed forces to defend its borders, respond to threats and protect its independence. The military also helps during natural disasters; in many countries, soldiers rescue people during floods and cyclones and deliver food and medicine. Without security, they argue, economic development and education cannot continue.

However, excessive military spending can be wasteful. Modern weapons are extremely expensive, and money spent on them cannot be used for schools, hospitals or infrastructure. In some countries, defence budgets are much larger than education budgets, even when there is no immediate threat. This may reflect political interests rather than real security needs. Large military budgets can also encourage arms races between neighbouring countries, which make the region less safe rather than more.

The right balance depends on each country's situation. A country facing a real threat of invasion may need to spend more on defence for a time. However, for most nations, especially developing ones, the greatest long-term threats are poverty, poor health, unemployment and lack of skills. Investing in education is therefore often the wisest way to build a strong and secure nation. Defence spending should be sufficient to protect the country but carefully controlled and transparent.

In conclusion, both education and defence are important responsibilities of government. However, while defence protects a country today, education **builds its future**. In most cases, governments should give higher priority to education while maintaining a reasonable and efficient defence budget.

### Public Transport versus Roads

As cities grow and more people own cars and motorcycles, traffic congestion has become one of the most serious urban problems in the world. Governments must decide how to spend their transport budgets: should they build more and wider roads, or should they invest in public transport such as buses, trains and metro rail systems? This choice affects the economy, the environment and the daily lives of millions of people.

The main argument for public transport is its **efficiency**. A single train or bus can carry many more people than private cars using the same amount of road space. A metro line can move tens of thousands of passengers per hour without adding a single car to the streets. When more people use public transport, traffic congestion falls, journeys become faster and **air pollution** is reduced, which improves public health. In cities such as Dhaka, the opening of metro rail has given many commuters a faster and more reliable way to travel.

Public transport is also **fairer**. Many people, including students, elderly people, people with disabilities and low-income workers, cannot afford a car or cannot drive. Affordable public transport allows them to reach schools, jobs, hospitals and markets. In this way, it reduces inequality and gives everyone access to opportunities in the city.

In contrast, building more roads often fails to solve congestion in the long term. New and wider roads may reduce traffic for a short time, but they also encourage more people to drive, and soon the roads become crowded again. This effect has been observed in many cities around the world. Roads also take up large areas of valuable land and increase noise and pollution.

However, roads remain essential in many situations. In rural areas, where populations are spread out, it is not practical to build railways to every village, so good roads are needed to connect people to towns, markets and hospitals. Roads are also vital for transporting goods by lorry, for emergency services and for buses themselves. Poor roads can slow economic development and make life difficult for rural communities.

Public transport also has disadvantages. Metro and railway systems are extremely **expensive to build** and can take many years to complete, causing disruption during construction. They also require continuous funding for maintenance and staff. If services are unreliable, dirty or overcrowded, people will continue to prefer private cars.

The best approach is to combine both. In large cities, governments should make public transport their main priority, investing in reliable, affordable and connected systems of buses, trains and safe footpaths. At the same time, they should maintain a good road network, particularly in rural areas and for freight. Measures such as bus lanes and parking charges can also encourage people to leave their cars at home.

In conclusion, public transport offers a more efficient, cleaner and fairer solution to urban transport problems than simply building more roads. However, roads remain necessary, and a **balanced transport policy** is the key to a successful modern city.

### Arts versus Science Funding

Governments fund many activities that are not strictly essential for survival, including the arts and scientific research. With limited budgets, some people argue that governments should focus on science and technology, which directly support economic growth and health, rather than on the arts, which they see as a luxury. Others believe that the arts are just as important to a healthy society.

The case for science funding is strong. Scientific research has produced vaccines, medicines, clean energy, computers and countless other inventions that have improved human life. Countries that invest in **science and technology** tend to develop high-skilled industries, create well-paid jobs and become more competitive in the global economy. Science is also essential for solving major challenges such as climate change, disease and food security. Because many of these benefits are practical and measurable, it is easy to see why governments prioritise science.

By contrast, the benefits of the arts are often less visible, but they are still significant. The arts, including music, literature, painting, theatre, dance and film, help to **preserve a nation's culture and identity**. They tell the story of a people's history, values and experiences, and pass them on to future generations. In an age of globalisation, when foreign entertainment strongly influences young people, supporting local art helps to protect cultural diversity.

The arts also have economic value. Museums, galleries, festivals and theatres attract tourists, who spend money on hotels, restaurants and transport. The creative industries, such as film, music, design and advertising, employ large numbers of people. In addition, the arts **improve quality of life**. Music, art and literature help people relax, express their emotions and understand others, and community arts projects can bring people together and reduce social isolation.

Some argue that artists should support themselves through the market, like any other business. However, many valuable art forms, especially traditional ones such as folk music, crafts and classical dance, cannot survive on ticket sales alone. Most artists earn low incomes, and without some public support, these traditions may disappear. On the other hand, government funding should be distributed fairly and transparently, and it should not be used to promote only art that supports the government's views.

Science, too, can receive funding from private companies, which often invest heavily in research that may be profitable. Basic research and art, however, rarely attract enough private money, which is why public funding is important for both.

The real choice, therefore, is not between science and the arts but about how much to give each. Science deserves strong support because of its practical benefits, but a modest arts budget brings large cultural, social and economic returns. Both contribute to a well-rounded society: science helps us live longer, while the arts help us **live meaningfully**.

In conclusion, governments should not abandon arts funding in favour of science. A balanced approach, with significant investment in research and a reasonable budget for the arts, will create a society that is both prosperous and culturally rich.

### Healthcare and Public Services

Public services such as hospitals, schools, water supply, sanitation, police and public transport are the backbone of a modern society. Governments are responsible for providing or regulating these services, and they usually make up a large part of public spending. The quality of public services has a direct effect on citizens' health, opportunities and trust in their government.

Healthcare is often seen as the most important public service. When people are ill, they cannot work, study or care for their families. A strong **public healthcare system** ensures that everyone, regardless of income, can receive treatment. This is especially important in developing countries, where many people cannot afford private hospitals and a serious illness can push a family into poverty. Investment in **preventive healthcare**, such as vaccinations, clean water and health education, is particularly cost-effective, because it stops diseases before they become expensive to treat.

Other basic services are equally vital. Clean water and proper sanitation prevent the spread of infectious diseases. Reliable electricity and internet access allow businesses to grow and students to learn. Effective police and courts protect people's safety and property. When these services work well, people are healthier, more productive and more confident about the future.

However, providing high-quality public services is expensive, and costs continue to rise. Populations are growing and ageing, medical technology is becoming more advanced, and citizens expect better services. Governments must therefore decide how to raise enough money, usually through taxes, and how to spend it efficiently.

A major problem in many countries is **inefficiency and corruption**. Money intended for hospitals or schools may be wasted on unnecessary bureaucracy, poorly managed projects or stolen by corrupt officials. As a result, public hospitals may be overcrowded and lack medicines, and schools may lack teachers and books, even when large budgets have been approved. Rural areas often receive fewer and lower-quality services than cities.

Some people argue that private companies could provide some services more efficiently. Private hospitals and schools often have better facilities, and competition can encourage higher quality. However, private services are usually more expensive, so poor people may be excluded. For essential services such as basic healthcare, education and water, most experts agree that governments must ensure **access for all**, even if private providers play a supporting role.

To improve public services, governments can increase transparency by publishing budgets and results, use technology to reduce paperwork and corruption, train and fairly pay public workers, and give priority to underserved rural areas. Citizens can also play a role by giving feedback and holding officials accountable.

In conclusion, healthcare and other public services are essential for a healthy, fair and productive society. Governments must invest adequately in them, but just as importantly, they must spend public money **efficiently and honestly**, so that every citizen receives the services they need.

### Space Exploration

Since the first satellite was launched into orbit in the 1950s, space exploration has fascinated people around the world. Today, several countries and private companies spend large sums of money on rockets, satellites, space stations and missions to the Moon and Mars. However, many people question whether this spending is justified when there are so many urgent problems on Earth.

Critics argue that space exploration is a **waste of resources**. Millions of people still live in extreme poverty, without enough food, clean water, healthcare or education. The cost of a single major space mission could build many hospitals or schools. From this point of view, it seems wrong for governments to spend money exploring distant planets while their own citizens are suffering. Critics also point out that space programmes mainly benefit wealthy countries and companies, and that the search for life on other planets has little direct effect on ordinary people's lives.

There are also concerns about safety and the environment. Rocket launches produce pollution, and thousands of pieces of old satellites and rockets, known as space debris, now orbit the Earth, creating a risk of collisions. Some scientists also worry that trying to contact intelligent life on other planets could be dangerous if it attracted unfriendly attention, although others think this risk is very small.

However, supporters argue that space exploration brings **enormous practical benefits**. Satellites, which were developed through space programmes, are now essential for weather forecasting, GPS navigation, television, internet and mobile communication. Weather satellites help predict cyclones and floods, giving people time to evacuate and saving many lives in countries such as Bangladesh. Satellites also help farmers monitor crops, scientists track climate change and governments respond to disasters.

Space research has also led to many **technological innovations** that are now used on Earth, including improvements in medical imaging, water purification, materials and computer technology. In addition, space exploration inspires young people to study science, technology, engineering and mathematics, which benefits the whole economy.

In the long term, some argue that exploring space may be essential for humanity's survival. Learning about other planets helps scientists understand Earth's own climate and geology, and one day, humans may need resources from space or even new places to live.

A balanced view recognises both sides. Space exploration should not take priority over basic human needs, but it does not have to be a choice between the two. Space budgets are usually a small share of total government spending, and countries can reduce costs by **cooperating internationally**, as they do on the International Space Station. Private companies are also investing in space, which reduces the burden on taxpayers.

In conclusion, although space exploration is expensive and problems on Earth are urgent, it brings valuable benefits in communication, disaster warning, technology and scientific knowledge. With sensible budgets and international cooperation, it can be a worthwhile investment in **humanity's future**.

### Sports Facilities and Hosting Big Events

Many countries compete to host major international sporting events, such as the Olympic Games, the football World Cup or the cricket World Cup. Governments also spend money on stadiums, sports centres and training programmes for athletes. Supporters believe that such investments bring national pride, economic growth and healthier lifestyles, while critics argue that they are often too expensive and bring fewer benefits than promised.

Hosting a major event can bring several advantages. It puts the host country **in the international spotlight**, improving its image and attracting tourists and foreign investors. During the event, visitors spend money on hotels, restaurants, transport and souvenirs, which supports local businesses and creates temporary jobs. Governments often use the event as an opportunity to **upgrade infrastructure**, building new roads, metro lines, airports and hotels that can benefit residents for many years.

Big events can also create a sense of **national pride and unity**. When a country successfully organises a world-class tournament, citizens feel proud, and people of different backgrounds come together to support their teams. In addition, seeing top athletes compete can inspire young people to take up sport, which may improve public health.

However, the costs of hosting major events are often enormous, and they frequently exceed the original budget. Stadiums, athletes' villages and security systems require huge amounts of public money, which could otherwise be spent on schools, hospitals or housing. After the event, some stadiums are rarely used and become expensive to maintain, a problem sometimes called "white elephants". Several host cities have been left with large debts.

There can also be social costs. In some cases, poor communities have been moved from their homes to make space for new facilities, and prices for housing and services rise in host cities. Much of the profit from tourism and broadcasting may go to international sports organisations and large companies rather than to local people.

Regular sports facilities for the public are a different matter. Local sports grounds, swimming pools, parks and community centres are relatively cheap and benefit large numbers of people every day. They encourage children and adults to exercise, reduce health problems, and give young people a positive way to spend their free time, which can reduce crime. Many experts believe that this kind of **grassroots sport** offers better value than hosting one expensive event.

Countries considering hosting a major event should therefore plan carefully. They should use existing facilities where possible, design new buildings that can be used by the community afterwards, keep budgets transparent and ensure that local people share the benefits.

In conclusion, hosting international sporting events can boost a country's image, economy and national pride, but the costs and risks are high. Governments should be cautious about such projects and give priority to **affordable community sports facilities** that improve the health and well-being of ordinary citizens every day.

### Welfare and Poverty

Every society has people who cannot fully support themselves, such as unemployed workers, people with disabilities, elderly people without pensions and families living in poverty. Many governments provide **social welfare**, including unemployment benefits, pensions, food assistance and free healthcare, to help them. However, there is ongoing debate about how much welfare governments should provide and what form it should take.

The main argument for welfare is that it acts as a **safety net**. People can lose their jobs, become seriously ill or suffer accidents through no fault of their own. Without support, they may fall into deep poverty, lose their homes or be unable to feed their children. Welfare protects them during difficult periods and allows them to recover and return to work. It also reduces inequality and can help to lower crime, because people in desperate situations are more likely to break the law.

Welfare also supports the economy. When people receive benefits, they continue to spend money on food, clothing and other basic goods, which helps local businesses during economic downturns. Programmes that support children's nutrition and education, such as free school meals or small cash payments to poor families on condition that children attend school, can improve the next generation's health and skills.

However, critics argue that generous welfare can **reduce the motivation to work**. If people can receive almost as much money from benefits as from a low-paid job, some may choose not to work. Welfare is also expensive, and high spending requires high taxes, which may discourage investment and economic growth. In addition, welfare systems can suffer from fraud and inefficiency, and in some countries benefits do not reach the people who need them most.

There is also debate about the best way to help homeless people. Some suggest simply giving them money, which respects their freedom to decide what they need. Others argue that cash alone does not solve the deeper causes of homelessness, such as unemployment, mental illness, addiction or family breakdown. Providing housing, healthcare, training and counselling may offer a more **lasting solution**.

Many experts believe that the most effective welfare systems combine support with opportunity. Instead of only giving money, governments can offer job training, help with finding work, affordable childcare and incentives that make work pay better than benefits. Support should be targeted at those in genuine need, and systems should be transparent to reduce fraud.

In developing countries, where formal welfare systems may be limited, community organisations, charities and families also play an important role, and programmes such as microcredit have helped many poor people start small businesses.

In conclusion, welfare is essential to protect vulnerable people and maintain a fair society, but it must be designed carefully. The best approach is to **give people a hand up, not just a handout**, combining financial support with education, training and opportunities to become independent.

### Foreign Aid

Foreign aid refers to money, goods and expertise that richer countries and international organisations give to poorer countries. Aid is used for many purposes, such as responding to natural disasters, fighting disease, building infrastructure and supporting education. While aid has helped millions of people, there is a long-running debate about how effective it is and what form it should take.

Aid can be extremely valuable in emergencies. After earthquakes, floods, cyclones or wars, international aid provides food, clean water, medicine and shelter that **save lives**. Long-term aid has also achieved important successes, such as vaccination programmes that have greatly reduced deadly diseases, and support for schools that has allowed millions of children to receive an education.

Aid for **infrastructure** can be particularly important. Many developing countries lack good roads, bridges, electricity, ports and water systems. Poor infrastructure makes it difficult for farmers to sell their crops, for businesses to grow and for children to reach school. A new bridge or road can connect isolated villages to markets and hospitals, transforming people's lives. For this reason, some argue that infrastructure should be the first priority for foreign aid.

However, aid also faces serious problems. **Corruption** is a major concern: money may be stolen by officials or spent on projects that benefit powerful people rather than the poor. Aid can also create dependence, discouraging governments from developing their own tax systems and solutions. Sometimes, donor countries give aid with conditions that serve their own political or business interests, such as requiring the recipient to buy goods from the donor country.

There is also debate about whether **financial aid** or **practical aid** is more useful. Money allows countries to fund large projects quickly and respond flexibly to their own priorities. However, practical aid, such as training doctors, teachers and engineers, sharing technology and advising on agriculture or water management, builds skills that remain in the country long after the aid ends. As the saying goes, it is better to teach a person to fish than to give them a fish.

Some people also argue that rich countries should focus on their own problems, such as poverty and unemployment at home, rather than giving money abroad. However, aid usually makes up only a small part of rich countries' budgets, and a more stable and prosperous world benefits everyone through trade, security and reduced migration pressures.

To make aid more effective, it should be transparent, carefully monitored and designed together with local communities, so that it meets real needs. Aid should aim to build local capacity, combining funding with training, and it should support honest governance.

In conclusion, foreign aid can save lives and support development, especially when it is used for infrastructure, health and education. However, to be truly effective, aid must avoid corruption and dependence and focus on building **long-term self-reliance** in the countries that receive it.

### Taxation

Taxes are the main way that governments pay for public services such as schools, hospitals, roads, police and defence. Although few people enjoy paying taxes, a fair and efficient tax system is essential for a functioning society. Questions about who should pay, how much, and what taxes should be used for are central to many political debates.

The most basic argument for taxation is that it allows governments to provide **public goods** that individuals could not provide alone. A single person cannot build a national highway or a public hospital, but when millions of people contribute through taxes, these services become possible. Taxes also allow governments to support vulnerable people, invest in the future and respond to emergencies.

Many countries use a **progressive tax system**, in which people with higher incomes pay a higher percentage of their income in tax. Supporters argue that this is fair, because wealthy people can afford to contribute more without suffering hardship, while poor people need every taka they earn for basic needs. Progressive taxation can also reduce inequality, which many believe leads to a more stable and peaceful society.

Governments also use taxes to **influence behaviour**. Taxes on tobacco, alcohol and sugary drinks discourage unhealthy habits, while taxes on fuel and pollution can encourage cleaner energy. At the same time, tax reductions can be used to encourage positive activities, such as investing in new businesses or installing solar panels.

However, taxation also has disadvantages. Very high taxes may discourage people from working harder or starting businesses, and they may cause wealthy individuals and companies to move their money or operations to countries with lower taxes. Some people feel that high taxes punish success. In addition, if citizens believe that their taxes are wasted or stolen through corruption, they may lose trust in the government and try to avoid paying.

**Tax evasion** is a serious problem in many countries, including developing ones. When wealthy people and large companies avoid paying their share, the burden falls more heavily on ordinary workers, and governments have less money for public services. Improving tax collection, using digital systems and closing loopholes can increase revenue without raising tax rates.

The key to a successful tax system is fairness and transparency. Citizens are more willing to pay taxes when they can see that their money is spent wisely on schools, hospitals and roads. Governments should therefore publish clear information about how tax money is used and fight corruption strongly.

In conclusion, taxation is necessary to fund the public services that everyone depends on. A fair system, in which those who earn more contribute more, combined with honest and efficient spending, can **build public trust** and support a prosperous and equal society.

### Rural Development

In many developing countries, including Bangladesh, a large part of the population still lives in rural areas. Yet villages often have fewer services and opportunities than cities. Roads may be poor, electricity and internet unreliable, schools and hospitals far away and good jobs scarce. As a result, many young people move to cities in search of work. **Rural development** aims to improve life in the countryside and reduce the gap between rural and urban areas.

One of the most important areas of rural development is **infrastructure**. Good roads and bridges allow farmers to transport their crops to markets quickly, reducing waste and increasing their income. They also make it easier for children to reach schools and for patients to reach hospitals. Reliable electricity, including solar power in remote areas, allows families to study at night, run small businesses and use modern equipment.

Access to the internet is increasingly important. With a stable connection, rural students can take online courses, farmers can check market prices and weather forecasts, and small businesses can sell products online. Digital services, such as mobile banking, have already transformed many villages by making it easier to send and save money.

Rural development also involves improving **public services**. Many villages have a shortage of qualified teachers and doctors, because professionals prefer to live in cities. Governments can offer incentives, such as higher salaries, housing and faster promotion, to encourage them to work in rural areas. Mobile clinics and telemedicine can also bring healthcare to remote communities.

Creating jobs outside agriculture is another key goal. Small industries, food processing, crafts, tourism and digital work can provide employment, so that young people do not have to leave their villages. Training programmes and small loans can help rural people start their own businesses.

Agriculture itself can also be modernised. Training farmers in better techniques, providing quality seeds and affordable equipment, and building storage facilities and cold chains can reduce crop losses and increase incomes. When farmers can store their harvest and sell it at a fair price instead of being forced to sell immediately, their families become more secure.

There are strong reasons to invest in rural development. It improves the lives of millions of people and reduces poverty. It also reduces pressure on overcrowded cities, where rapid migration has led to slums, traffic and pollution. In addition, it supports agriculture, which is essential for national food security.

However, rural development can be expensive and slow, because populations are spread over large areas, and some projects may benefit relatively few people. Governments must therefore plan carefully, prioritising projects with the greatest impact and involving local communities in decision-making so that projects meet their real needs.

In conclusion, rural development is essential for a fair and balanced society. By investing in infrastructure, services and jobs in the countryside, governments can **reduce inequality**, slow migration to cities and give rural people the opportunity to build a good life **without leaving their homes**.

### Museums, Libraries and Heritage

Museums, libraries, historic buildings and cultural sites preserve a nation's memory. They contain objects, documents, artworks and buildings that tell the story of a people's past. Many governments fund these institutions, but in times of limited budgets, some people question whether public money should be spent on preserving the past rather than on present needs.

The main argument for supporting museums and heritage is that they **preserve history and identity**. Historic sites and museum collections help people understand where they come from and how their society developed. In Bangladesh, for example, museums about the Language Movement and the Liberation War help young people learn about the sacrifices that shaped their nation. Without such institutions, important parts of history could be forgotten or lost.

Museums and libraries are also important **educational resources**. School trips to museums make history and science come alive in a way that textbooks cannot. Public libraries give everyone, regardless of income, free access to books, newspapers, computers and the internet, which supports lifelong learning. For students from poor families, a library may be the only quiet place to study.

Heritage sites also bring **economic benefits** through tourism. Ancient temples, mosques, palaces and archaeological sites attract visitors from home and abroad, who spend money on transport, hotels, food and souvenirs. This creates jobs for local people and encourages the protection of the surrounding area.

However, there are arguments against high spending on heritage. Maintaining old buildings and large collections is expensive, and some museums attract few visitors. In growing cities, some people argue that old buildings should be replaced with modern ones that meet current needs, such as housing or offices. In addition, some argue that museums should charge entry fees or find private sponsors rather than relying on taxpayers.

There is also debate about what museums should show. Some believe museums should focus mainly on local artists and national history, to protect local culture. Others argue that international exhibitions help people understand other cultures and attract more visitors.

Modern technology offers new opportunities. Museums and libraries can digitise their collections so that people anywhere can access them online, and interactive displays can attract younger visitors. However, digital copies cannot fully replace the experience of seeing a real historical object or visiting an ancient site.

A balanced approach is best. Governments should protect the most important heritage sites and support museums and libraries, especially those with educational value, while encouraging them to raise additional income through tickets, shops, events and sponsorship.

In conclusion, museums, libraries and heritage sites are valuable investments in education, identity and tourism. Although they must be managed efficiently, they help societies **learn from the past** and pass their culture on to future generations.

## Part 5 — Sentence Structure Toolkit {: .part}

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Paragraph mix formula:** প্রতিটি body paragraph-এ রাখার চেষ্টা করো → **1টি long simple + 1–2টি compound + 2–3টি complex + 1টি compound-complex** sentence। 5–6টি বাক্য × গড়ে 20 শব্দ = **100–120 words**।

</div>

#### 1. Long simple sentence (rich noun phrase)

**Formula:** [long noun phrase] + verb + object
*একটাই clause, কিন্তু subject-টা বড় ও তথ্যপূর্ণ।*

- The construction of a modern metro rail system in a crowded city **requires** huge public investment.
- Government spending on schools, hospitals and roads **affects** the daily lives of every citizen.

#### 2. Compound with *and / but / so / yet*

- Hosting the Olympic Games brings prestige, **but** it can leave a country in debt.
- Rural roads are poor, **so** farmers struggle to sell their crops.

#### 3. Compound with semicolon + linker

- Space research is expensive**; however,** it has given us weather satellites.
- Corruption wastes public money**; therefore,** budgets should be published online.

#### 4. Complex — concession (*Although / Even though / While*)

- **Although** defence is important, education builds a country's future.
- **Even though** arts funding is small, it supports many traditional artists.

#### 5. Complex — reason (*Because / Since / As*)

- **Since** public transport serves people without cars, it reduces inequality.
- **Because** taxes are paid by everyone, governments must spend them wisely.

#### 6. Complex — condition (*If / Unless / Provided that*)

- **If** governments invest in villages, fewer young people **will** move to the cities.
- Foreign aid can be effective, **provided that** it is carefully monitored.

#### 7. Relative clause (*which / who / where / whose*)

- Satellites, **which** were developed through space programmes, now provide weather forecasts.
- Artists **whose** work preserves traditional culture deserve public support.

#### 8. Compound-complex

**Formula:** Although/Because/If + clause, Clause A, and/but Clause B

- **Although** stadiums are built for big events, many are rarely used afterwards, **and** maintaining them is costly.
- **If** taxes are spent honestly, citizens trust the government, **and** they are more willing to pay.

#### 9. Participle phrase

- **Funded** by taxpayers, public hospitals must serve everyone equally.
- **Faced** with limited budgets, governments must set clear priorities.

#### 10. *Not only … but also …*

- Public libraries **not only** provide free books **but also** offer a quiet place to study.
- Good roads **not only** help farmers **but also** connect villages to hospitals.

#### 11. *The more …, the more …*

- **The more** a country invests in education, **the more** productive its workforce becomes.
- **The wider** the roads, **the more** cars they attract.

#### 12. Cleft sentence (emphasis)

- **It is** education, not weapons, **that** creates long-term security.
- **What** taxpayers want most **is** value for their money.

#### 13. Impersonal passive

- **It is often argued that** space exploration is a waste of money.
- **It is widely accepted that** governments must provide basic services.

#### 14. Contrasting views

- **While some people believe that** the arts are a luxury, **others see** them as essential to national identity.
- **While** financial aid works quickly, **practical aid** builds lasting skills.

#### 15. Result / consequence

- Many host cities overspend on stadiums, **which means that** less money is left for schools.
- Rural areas lack good jobs. **As a result,** young people migrate to the cities.

#### অনুবাদের ৭টি ধাপ

1. **পুরো অনুচ্ছেদটি আগে পড়ো।** প্রতিটি শব্দ নয়, মূল idea-টা বোঝো।

7. **শব্দে শব্দে অনুবাদ করবে না।** অর্থটা স্বাভাবিক ইংরেজিতে প্রকাশ করো। ঘড়ি ধরো: প্রতিটি essay 40 মিনিটে।

#### Introduction (35–50 words)

প্রায়ই যুক্তি দেওয়া হয় যে শিল্পকলায় দেওয়া সরকারি অর্থ অপচয় হয় এবং তা অন্য কাজে ব্যবহার করা উচিত। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমি এই মতের সাথে সম্পূর্ণ দ্বিমত পোষণ করি, কারণ শিল্পকলা একটি জাতির সংস্কৃতি রক্ষা করে এবং অর্থনীতিকেও সহায়তা করে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — Idea 1 (100–120 words)

এই ধারণার সাথে আমার দ্বিমতের প্রধান কারণ হলো, শিল্পকলা একটি জাতির সংস্কৃতি ও পরিচয় (identity) রক্ষা করতে সাহায্য করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> এর কারণ হলো, সংগীত, সাহিত্য, নাটক ও ঐতিহ্যবাহী কারুশিল্প একটি দেশের ইতিহাস ও মূল্যবোধ ধরে রাখে, এবং এগুলো প্রতিটি প্রজন্মকে আগের প্রজন্মের সাথে যুক্ত করে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> বিদেশি বিনোদন যখন তরুণদের ওপর জোরালো প্রভাব ফেলে, তখন সরকারি সহায়তা সবচেয়ে বেশি দরকার হয়; ফলে স্থানীয় ঐতিহ্য বিশ্বসংস্কৃতির পাশাপাশি টিকে থাকতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, সরকারি অনুদান (grant) একটি লোকসংগীত দলকে স্কুলে স্কুলে গান পরিবেশন করতে সাহায্য করতে পারে, এবং এতে শিশুরা নিজেদের ঐতিহ্য সম্পর্কে জানতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound</span> তাই শিল্পকলায় ব্যয় করা অর্থ জাতীয় পরিচয়ের জন্য একটি বিনিয়োগ। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — Idea 2 (100–120 words)

আমার দ্বিমতের আরেকটি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ কারণ হলো, শিল্পকলা অর্থনীতিকেও সহায়তা করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> কোনো দেশের সাংস্কৃতিক জীবন যখন প্রাণবন্ত হয়, তখন দেশটি অর্থ উপার্জন করে, কারণ জাদুঘর, গ্যালারি, উৎসব ও থিয়েটার পর্যটক আকর্ষণ করে, যারা হোটেল, রেস্তোরাঁ ও যাতায়াতে টাকা খরচ করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যেমন, একটি ঐতিহ্যবাহী সংগীত উৎসব অনেক দেশ থেকে দর্শনার্থী আকর্ষণ করতে এবং স্থানীয় ব্যবসাকে সহায়তা করতে পারে, যা ওই এলাকার পরিবারগুলোর আয় তৈরি করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে এই অর্থ হাসপাতাল ও স্কুলে যাওয়া উচিত, শিল্পকলায় ব্যয় করা অর্থ তাদের বাজেটের তুলনায় খুবই সামান্য। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে শিল্পকলায় অর্থায়ন বাস্তব সুফল আনে, এবং একে জনগণের অর্থের অপচয় হিসেবে দেখা উচিত নয়। <span class="tag">Compound · Passive</span>

#### Conclusion (35–55 words)

#### Introduction (35–45 words)

কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে মহাকাশ অনুসন্ধান মূল্যবান সম্পদের অপচয়, অন্যদিকে অনেকে বিশ্বাস করেন যে এটি মানবজাতির জন্য অপরিহার্য। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমি শেষের মতটিকে সম্পূর্ণ সমর্থন করি, কারণ মহাকাশ গবেষণা পৃথিবীর জীবনে ব্যবহারিক সুফল নিয়ে আসে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

একদিকে, যারা মহাকাশ অনুসন্ধানের বিরোধিতা করেন, তারা যুক্তি দেন যে এতে বিপুল অর্থ ব্যয় হয়, যা জরুরি সমস্যা সমাধানে ব্যবহার করা যেত। <span class="tag">Complex</span> লক্ষ লক্ষ মানুষ এখনো চরম দারিদ্র্যে বাস করে, যাদের যথেষ্ট খাবার, বিশুদ্ধ পানি, চিকিৎসা বা শিক্ষা নেই। <span class="tag">Complex</span> এই চাহিদাগুলো পূরণ না হলেও দূরের গ্রহের পেছনে বিশাল বাজেট ব্যয় করা হয়; ফলে মনে হয় সরকারের অগ্রাধিকার ভুল (the wrong priorities)। <span class="tag">Compound · Passive</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, খরাপীড়িত অঞ্চলের একজন কৃষক দূরের কোনো গ্রহের নতুন ছবি থেকে সরাসরি কিছুই পান না, এবং এটি এই ব্যয়কে অন্যায্য মনে করাতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound</span> এ কারণে অনেকে বিশ্বাস করেন যে মহাকাশ অনুসন্ধান সম্পদের অপচয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — আমার মত (90–120 words)

অন্যদিকে, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে মহাকাশ অনুসন্ধান অপরিহার্য, কারণ এটি দৈনন্দিন জীবনে অনেক ব্যবহারিক সুফল এনেছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ঝড় ও বন্যা যখন মানুষকে বিপদে ফেলে, তখন মহাকাশ কর্মসূচির মাধ্যমে তৈরি স্যাটেলাইট আবহাওয়ার পূর্বাভাস দেয়, এবং এই পূর্বাভাস পরিবারগুলোকে প্রস্তুত হওয়ার সময় দেয়। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, আবহাওয়া স্যাটেলাইট কয়েক দিন আগেই বাংলাদেশের উপকূলীয় জনগোষ্ঠীকে ঘূর্ণিঝড়ের সতর্কবার্তা দিতে পারে, এবং এই আগাম সতর্কতা (early warnings) অনেক জীবন বাঁচাতে সাহায্য করেছে। <span class="tag">Compound</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে এই অর্থ গরিব মানুষের সাহায্যে ব্যয় করা উচিত, এই স্যাটেলাইটগুলো গরিব জনগোষ্ঠীকেই সরাসরি রক্ষা করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে মহাকাশ গবেষণায় অর্থ ব্যয় করা মানবজাতির ভবিষ্যতের জন্য একটি বিচক্ষণ বিনিয়োগ। <span class="tag">Simple · Verb+ing</span>

#### Conclusion (35–45 words)

পরিশেষে, মহাকাশ অনুসন্ধান অর্থের অপচয় নাকি মানবজাতির জন্য প্রয়োজনীয়, এ নিয়ে মানুষের মধ্যে ভিন্ন ভিন্ন মত আছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও দারিদ্র্যের মতো সমস্যাগুলো জরুরি, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে এই গবেষণা অপরিহার্য, কারণ এর সুফল ইতিমধ্যেই পৃথিবীতে মানুষের জীবন রক্ষা করছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

### Essay 3 — Advantages and Disadvantages

<div class="question">Many governments spend large amounts of money on building new roads, while others prefer to invest in public transport. What are the advantages and disadvantages of investing in public transport rather than roads?</div>

**অবস্থান:** প্রশ্নে মতামত চাওয়া হয়নি, তাই মতামত বা plan sentence নেই। Although ও While বাক্যে আগে অসুবিধা, comma-র পরে সুবিধা।<br>
**কাঠামো:** IELTS_Advantages_Disadvantages_Essay_Guide.md অনুযায়ী — Type 1 (মতামত চাওয়া হয়নি) — Although-introduction, প্রতিটি body paragraph-এ ঠিক 7টি বাক্য, While-conclusion। মতামত বা "This essay will…" বাক্য নেই। মোট 310–340 শব্দ।

**পরিকল্পনা (Plan)**

- **Introduction (30–40):** paraphrase → Although + অসুবিধা (ব্যয়বহুল, সময়সাপেক্ষ), + সুবিধা (দক্ষ পরিবহন, গাড়িহীন মানুষের সেবা)। মতামত নেই।
- **Body Paragraph 1 (120–135) — সুবিধা, 7টি বাক্য:** বিপুল মানুষ পরিবহন → একই রাস্তায় বেশি যাত্রী → উদাহরণ: ঢাকার মেট্রো → দূষণ কমে ও দরিদ্রদের সেবা → কম ভাড়া, বৈষম্য কমে → Overall…
- **Body Paragraph 2 (120–135) — অসুবিধা, 7টি বাক্য:** ব্যয়বহুল ও দীর্ঘ নির্মাণ → রাস্তা বন্ধ, যানজট → উদাহরণ: মেট্রো লাইনের পাশের বাসিন্দা → গ্রামে রাস্তা লাগে → কৃষকের ফসল → Overall, these…
- **Conclusion (25–35):** নতুন paraphrase → While + অসুবিধা, + সুবিধা। মতামত নেই।

#### Introduction (30–40 words)

সরকারকে সিদ্ধান্ত নিতে হয় যে তারা পরিবহন বাজেট নতুন রাস্তায় খরচ করবে, নাকি বাস, ট্রেন ও মেট্রোরেলে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও গণপরিবহন নির্মাণ ব্যয়বহুল ও সময়সাপেক্ষ, এটি দক্ষতার সাথে মানুষ পরিবহন করে এবং যাদের গাড়ি নেই তাদের সেবা দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — সুবিধা (120–135 words)

এতে কোনো সন্দেহ নেই যে গণপরিবহনে বিনিয়োগ শহরের বাসিন্দাদের জন্য উল্লেখযোগ্য সুবিধা বয়ে আনে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> প্রথমত, সবচেয়ে বড় সুবিধা হলো, গণপরিবহন দক্ষতার সাথে বিপুল সংখ্যক মানুষকে এক জায়গা থেকে আরেক জায়গায় নিয়ে যায়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> এর কারণ হলো, একই পরিমাণ রাস্তার জায়গা ব্যবহার করে একটি ট্রেন বা বাস ব্যক্তিগত গাড়ির চেয়ে অনেক বেশি মানুষ বহন করতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, ঢাকার একটি মেট্রো ট্রেন শত শত ব্যক্তিগত গাড়ির সমান যাত্রী বহন করতে পারে, এবং এতে নিচের রাস্তাগুলোতে যানজট কমে। <span class="tag">Compound</span> এছাড়া, গণপরিবহন শুধু বায়ুদূষণই কমায় না, যাদের গাড়ি কেনার সামর্থ্য নেই তাদেরও সেবা দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> কম ভাড়ার ফলে শিক্ষার্থী, বয়স্ক মানুষ ও নিম্ন আয়ের কর্মীরা স্কুল, কর্মস্থল ও হাসপাতালে পৌঁছাতে পারে, যা বৈষম্য (inequality) কমায়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> সব মিলিয়ে, গণপরিবহন শহরের যাতায়াতকে সবার জন্য দ্রুত, পরিচ্ছন্ন ও ন্যায্য করে তোলে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — অসুবিধা (120–135 words)

অন্যদিকে, এটি অস্বীকার করা যায় না যে গণপরিবহনে বিনিয়োগের কিছু অসুবিধাও আছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> প্রথমত, আধুনিক ব্যবস্থা নির্মাণ অত্যন্ত ব্যয়বহুল (expensive to build), এবং তা শেষ হতে অনেক বছর লাগতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound</span> নির্মাণকাজের জন্য প্রায়ই দীর্ঘ সময় রাস্তা বন্ধ থাকে; ফলে নতুন ব্যবস্থা চালু হওয়ার আগে যানজট আরও খারাপ হয়। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> যেমন, নতুন মেট্রো লাইনের পথের পাশে যারা বাস করেন, লাইনটি নির্মাণের সময় তাদের বছরের পর বছর যানজট, ধুলা ও শব্দের কষ্ট সহ্য করতে হতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex · Passive</span> দ্বিতীয়ত, রাস্তার প্রয়োজনীয়তা একটি গুরুতর বিষয় থেকেই যায়, কারণ বাস ও ট্রেন প্রতিটি গ্রামে পৌঁছাতে পারে না। <span class="tag">Complex</span> সরকার যদি রাস্তায় খুব কম খরচ করে, তাহলে কৃষকেরা ফসল বাজারে নিতে হিমশিম খেতে পারেন, এবং ফলস্বরূপ গ্রামাঞ্চল আরও পিছিয়ে পড়তে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> সব মিলিয়ে, এই আর্থিক ও বাস্তব সমস্যাগুলো বোঝায় যে গণপরিবহন কখনো রাস্তার জায়গা পুরোপুরি নিতে পারে না। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Conclusion (25–35 words)

পরিশেষে, রাস্তা নাকি গণপরিবহন — এই বাছাই সরকারের জন্য একটি বড় সিদ্ধান্ত। <span class="tag">Simple</span> যদিও (While) গণপরিবহন ব্যয়বহুল এবং সব এলাকায় সেবা দিতে পারে না, এটি বেশি মানুষ বহন করে এবং দরিদ্রদের প্রতি বেশি ন্যায্য। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

</div>

- ❌ *spend money for education* → ✅ spend money **on** education; invest **in** education
- ❌ *budget of education is less* → ✅ the education budget is **small / insufficient**
- ❌ *Arts is important.* → ✅ **The arts are** important (meaning music, painting, theatre…).
- ❌ *If government will spend more…* → ✅ **If** the government **spends** more, … **will** …
- **"spend money" প্রতিশব্দ:** allocate funds to, invest in, fund, finance, devote resources to, channel money into

</div>

### Homework 1 — Double Question

<div class="question">In many countries, the government spends a large share of its budget on defence. Why do you think this is the case? Do you think more of this money should be spent on education instead?</div>

*Source: Practice question · ★ Government should spend more on education than defence (no IELTS Liz question on this exact topic)*

**Essay type:** Double Question — List + Opinion · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 = Q1 (List block) 90–120 · BP2 = Q2 (Opinion block) 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** Education vs Defence; Role of Government; Taxation

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- Q1 "কেন প্রতিরক্ষায় বেশি ব্যয়" কারণ জানতে চায়, তাই List block: 2টি কারণ (সীমান্ত নিরাপত্তা, দুর্যোগে সেনাবাহিনীর ভূমিকা) ও একটি উদাহরণ, "I" ছাড়া।
- Q2 পক্ষ বেছে নিতে বলে, তাই Opinion block: হ্যাঁ অথবা না, "আংশিকভাবে" নয়। N-L-F test দিয়ে একটি কারণ বেছে নাও।
- Opinion block: শিক্ষার দীর্ঘমেয়াদি সুফল (দক্ষ জনশক্তি) + একটি উদাহরণ + Response (Although some people argue that নিরাপত্তা আগে, [তোমার পক্ষ])।

</div>

**Try to use:** national security · defend its borders · military spending · the foundation of long-term development · a skilled workforce · pay for itself · give higher priority to · a reasonable defence budget

**Use ideas from:** Foreign Aid; Rural Development; Public Transport vs Roads

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- "first problem" একটি দৃঢ় দাবি। একটিই পক্ষ নাও: সম্পূর্ণ একমত বা সম্পূর্ণ দ্বিমত, আংশিক নয়।
