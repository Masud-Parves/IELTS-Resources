## Part 1 — Idea Generation Flow {: .part}

<div class="flow">Education → Cost &amp; Free Education → Inequality (Rich–Poor, Rural–Urban) → Online vs Classroom Learning → Exams &amp; Assessment → Teachers &amp; Parents → Academic vs Practical Skills → Technology in Schools → University &amp; Employment → Studying Abroad &amp; Languages → Arts, Sport &amp; PE → Discipline &amp; School Life → Lifelong Learning</div>

Education is the **most frequently tested** Task 2 topic. When you see an education question, run through this chain from left to right and ask: *"Does this question touch this area?"* Then **stop at the 2 links that fit best**. Those 2 links become your two body paragraphs.

**Memory trick — the 4 big questions of education:**

| Big question | Links in the chain |
|---|---|
| **1. Who pays, and who gets it?** | Cost & Free Education · Inequality · Online vs Classroom |
| **2. What and how do we learn?** | Exams · Academic vs Practical · Technology · Arts, Sport & PE · Languages |
| **3. Who guides the child?** | Teachers & Parents · Discipline & School Life |
| **4. What happens after school?** | University & Employment · Studying Abroad · Lifelong Learning |

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Example:** *"Should all levels of education, including university, be free?"*

Big question 1 → **Cost** ✔ (who pays: government / taxpayers / students) → **Inequality** ✔ (poor students can study) → Big question 4 → **University & Employment** ✔ (skilled workforce, but too many graduates for the jobs).
Choose **Inequality** for Body Paragraph 1 (why free education is good) and **Cost / Employment** for Body Paragraph 2 (the problems).

</div>

## Part 2 — Exam-Hall Brainstorming System {: .part}

### 2A. The 5-Minute Plan

| Minute | What to do | What you write on the question paper |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | **Decode the question.** Underline the topic words and the instruction words. Decide which of your five essay guides it uses (Part 2G). | e.g. *all levels / free / to what extent → Agree / Disagree guide* |
| 1–3 | **WHO + WHAT grid.** Who is affected? What is the effect? | 4–6 one-line notes |
| 3–4 | **Choose the 2 strongest ideas.** Add one reason and one example to each. | Idea 1 + why + example; Idea 2 + why + example |
| 4–5 | **Decide your position (one clear side, only if the question asks) and use the guide's word plan.** | Thesis / BP1 / BP2 / Conclusion in 4 lines |

### 2B. WHO is affected by education decisions?

- **Children / pupils** — workload, pressure, interest in learning, physical and mental health
- **Teenagers / secondary students** — exams, career choice, discipline, exchange programmes
- **University students** — tuition fees, debt, graduate jobs, studying abroad
- **Parents** — cost, involvement, pressure on children, values at home
- **Teachers** — workload, salary, motivation, classroom discipline, training
- **Schools and universities** — funding, curriculum, technology, facilities
- **Employers** — skilled workforce, practical skills, soft skills
- **Governments / taxpayers** — education budget, national development
- **Poor and rural families** — access to good schools, internet, qualified teachers
- **Adults and elderly people** — retraining, lifelong learning, staying active

### 2E. How One Idea Becomes a Full Paragraph (PEEL)

**P**oint → **E**xplain (why? how?) → **E**xample → **L**ink / Result

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### বাংলায় চিন্তা (Bangla thinking)

**Point:** বিনামূল্যে উচ্চশিক্ষা দরিদ্র শিক্ষার্থীদের সমান সুযোগ দেয়।
**Explain:** কারণ অনেক মেধাবী শিক্ষার্থী শুধু টাকার অভাবে বিশ্ববিদ্যালয়ে ভর্তি হতে পারে না।
**Example:** যেমন, একজন কৃষকের মেধাবী সন্তান বিনা খরচে পড়ার সুযোগ পেলে ডাক্তার বা প্রকৌশলী হতে পারে।
**Link/Result:** ফলে শুধু তার পরিবারই নয়, পুরো সমাজই উপকৃত হয়।

</div>

**The same idea in English (one paragraph):**

> **Point:** The most important argument for free higher education is that it gives every talented young person an equal opportunity.
> **Explain:** Many intelligent students never go to university simply because their families cannot afford the fees, which is a waste of their potential.
> **Example:** For example, if the son or daughter of a poor farmer can study medicine or engineering without paying tuition fees, they may become a doctor or an engineer.
> **Link:** As a result, not only their own family but the whole of society benefits from their skills.

Count it: about **85 words** from one idea. Add one more explanation or result sentence and you reach the **100–120 words** that one body paragraph needs.

### 2G. Essay-Type Paragraph Plans (your five essay guides)

| Essay type (guide) | Introduction | Body Paragraph 1 | Body Paragraph 2 | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Agree / Disagree** (*to what extent? / do you agree?*) | **35–50:** paraphrase + *I completely agree/disagree with this view because* Idea 1 and Idea 2. Never "partly". | **100–120:** Idea 1 — P E E X L | **100–120:** Idea 2 — P E X R L (*Although some people argue that …, [my side]*) | **35–55:** *In conclusion, I firmly believe that …* + both ideas in new words |
| **Discuss Both Views** (*some think X, others think Y*) | **35–45:** *While some people argue that [other view], others believe that [my view]. I completely support the latter view because …* | **90–120:** the view you do **not** support, no "I" — P E E X L | **90–120:** **your view** — P E X R L | **35–45:** *In conclusion, people hold different views on whether … While …, I firmly believe that …* |
| **Advantages – Disadvantages** | **40–50:** paraphrase + *Although* [side you do not support], [side you support] + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **30–40**, 2 sentences) | **120–135:** advantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **120–135:** disadvantages — 7 sentences (T P E X P E S) | **35–45:** *While …* + opinion only if asked (no opinion asked: **25–35**) |
| **Cause/Problem + Solution** | **30–40:** paraphrase + *This is mainly because C1 and C2, but S1 and S2 can tackle this problem.* No opinion. | **90–120:** 2 causes or problems — P E X P E L | **90–120:** 2 solutions, each fixing one cause (WHO + WHAT + HOW) | **30–40:** causes + solutions in new words |
| **Double Question** (*two questions*) | **30–40:** paraphrase + *[Answer 1], and [answer 2].* | **90–120:** answer to Q1 — List block (P E X P E L) or Opinion block (P E X R L) | **90–120:** answer to Q2 — List or Opinion block | **30–40:** *In conclusion,* answer 1 + answer 2 in new words |

*Full rules: `Writing/IELTS_*_Essay_Guide.md`. Questions asking only for solutions or only for problems use the Cause/Problem + Solution guide, Section 14: one idea in each body paragraph (P E E X L), no opinion.*

## Part 3 — Category-Wise Free Writing {: .part}

Each passage below is an **idea store**. Read it slowly, notice how each idea is explained and supported with an example, and pay attention to the **bold collocations**. After each passage, try to say 4–5 of its ideas aloud in your own words.

### Education and Cost: Should Education Be Free?

The question of who should pay for education is one of the most debated issues in modern society. In most countries, primary and secondary education are already free in state schools, because governments accept that every child needs basic literacy and numeracy. The real debate concerns higher education. Some people believe that university should also be free for everyone, while others argue that students, who gain the most from a degree, should pay at least part of the cost.

The strongest argument for free education is that it **provides equal opportunities**. Talent is found in every social class, but money is not. When universities charge high tuition fees, many intelligent young people from low-income families never apply, or they leave their studies early to support their families. If education is free, a bright child from a poor village has the same chance as a child from a wealthy city family to become a doctor, an engineer or a teacher. In this way, free education can help to **break the cycle of poverty**, because a single graduate can lift a whole family into a better life.

Free education also benefits the economy and society as a whole. A country with a **highly educated workforce** is more productive, more innovative and more attractive to foreign investors. Educated citizens are also more likely to be healthy, to obey the law and to take part in public life. From this point of view, the money spent on education is not a cost but a **long-term investment** that returns to the state through higher taxes and lower spending on welfare and crime.

However, free higher education is extremely expensive. Governments have limited budgets and must also pay for hospitals, roads, security and pensions. If they spend huge sums on universities, other public services may suffer, or taxes may have to rise for everyone, including people who never went to university. Critics also point out that free university places are often taken mainly by students from middle-class families, so poorer taxpayers may end up **subsidising the education** of the better-off.

Another concern is quality and motivation. When universities receive less money per student, classes may become overcrowded and facilities may decline. Some people also argue that students value what they pay for; if a course is completely free, a few students may not take it seriously, change courses frequently or take many years to graduate.

A balanced solution is possible. Primary and secondary education should **remain completely free** and of high quality, because they form the foundation for everything else. For higher education, governments can offer free places or generous **scholarships to students from low-income families**, low-interest loans that are repaid only when graduates earn a good salary, and free training in subjects the country urgently needs, such as nursing or engineering.

In conclusion, access to education should never depend on a family's income. Whether every university course should be free is less certain, but governments must make sure that no talented student is prevented from studying simply because they are poor.

### Education and Inequality: Rich and Poor, City and Village

Education is often described as the great equaliser, because it can give a child from any background the chance to build a better life. In reality, however, the quality of education that children receive often depends on where they are born. There is a wide gap between richer and poorer countries, and within many countries there is a similar gap between cities and rural areas, and between wealthy and poor families.

In poorer countries and rural regions, schools frequently **lack basic resources**. Classrooms may be overcrowded, with sixty or more pupils sharing one teacher, and there may be no science laboratory, library or reliable electricity. Many children have to walk long distances to school, and some leave early to work on farms or help at home. Girls in particular may be kept out of school because of poverty or traditional attitudes. As a result, millions of young people finish their education without strong reading, writing or mathematics skills.

One of the most serious problems is the **shortage of qualified teachers** in rural areas. Good teachers often prefer to work in cities, where salaries are higher, living conditions are better and their own children can attend good schools. Village schools are therefore left with too few teachers, or with teachers who have little training. Even where teachers are available, they may have to teach several subjects they did not study themselves.

Inequality also exists within the same city. Wealthy families can pay for private schools, private tutors, books, computers and educational trips, while poorer families cannot. Private tutoring has become so common in some countries that students who cannot afford it fall behind, even when they attend the same school. This means that education, instead of reducing inequality, can sometimes **widen the gap** between rich and poor.

There are several practical ways to reduce these problems. Governments can **offer incentives to teachers** who work in remote areas, such as higher salaries, free housing and faster promotion. Free school meals and small cash payments to poor families can encourage parents to keep their children, especially girls, in school. Technology can also help: online lessons and recorded lectures by excellent teachers can reach village classrooms, provided that schools have electricity and internet access.

At the international level, richer countries and organisations can support poorer nations by funding school buildings, training teachers and providing textbooks. However, this aid should be designed together with local communities, so that it meets real local needs and continues after the foreign funding ends. Ultimately, poorer countries also need to **give education a higher priority** in their own national budgets.

In conclusion, education can only be a ladder out of poverty if every child can actually reach it. Closing the gap between rich and poor, and between city and village, requires investment in teachers, schools and families, and it is one of the most effective ways to build a fairer society.

### Online Learning versus the Traditional Classroom

Over the past decade, online learning has grown from a small alternative into a major part of education. Universities offer complete degrees online, schools use digital platforms for homework and lessons, and millions of adults take short online courses in their free time. During periods when schools had to close, many students experienced online classes for months. This has raised an important question: can online education replace traditional classrooms?

Online learning has clear advantages. The first is **flexibility**. Students can study at a time and place that suits them, which is ideal for working adults, parents and people who live far from a university. Learners can also **study at their own pace**, pausing and replaying a recorded lecture until they understand it, rather than trying to keep up with a fast teacher in a crowded room. In addition, online courses are often cheaper, because students save money on travel, accommodation and printed materials.

Online education also gives students access to excellent teachers and resources from all over the world. A student in a small town can follow lectures by leading professors, join international discussion groups and use digital libraries that their local school could never afford. For people who are shy, disabled or unable to travel, this can **open up opportunities** that were previously closed to them.

Nevertheless, the traditional classroom offers things that online learning cannot easily replace. The most important is **face-to-face interaction**. In a real classroom, teachers can see immediately when a student is confused, bored or upset, and they can respond at once. Students learn from discussions, group projects and friendships, which build communication and teamwork skills that employers value highly. School also teaches children how to behave in a community, respect rules and resolve conflicts.

Discipline and motivation are other weaknesses of online learning. Studying alone at home requires strong **self-discipline**, and many learners, especially young children and teenagers, are easily distracted by social media, games and household noise. Completion rates for free online courses are often low, because no one checks whether students attend. Furthermore, online learning depends on technology; students without a good device or a stable internet connection may be left behind.

For practical subjects, such as medicine, engineering, science experiments or sport, physical presence is essential. A future surgeon cannot learn surgery only by watching videos, and a chemistry student needs to work in a real laboratory under supervision.

For these reasons, the most effective model for the future is probably **blended learning**, a combination of online and classroom teaching. Lectures and theory can be delivered online, while classroom time is used for discussion, practical work and personal support.

In conclusion, online learning is a valuable tool that has made education more flexible and accessible, particularly for adults. However, for children and for practical subjects, it should support rather than replace the traditional classroom, where human contact remains at the heart of learning.

### Exams, Assessment and Homework

Assessment is an essential part of education, because teachers, parents, universities and employers need to know what students have learned. For a long time, the main method of assessment has been the final written examination. Recently, however, many schools have started to replace or combine exams with **continuous assessment**, in which students are graded on projects, assignments, presentations and class work throughout the year.

Traditional exams have some important strengths. They are generally considered **fair and objective**, because all students answer the same questions under the same conditions, often without knowing who will mark their papers. This makes cheating and favouritism more difficult. Exams also encourage students to review a whole course and to remember key information, and they train students to work under time pressure, which is a useful skill in many jobs.

However, exams have serious weaknesses. They often **test memory rather than understanding**, so students may memorise answers the night before and forget everything a week later. A single exam can also be unfair: a student who works hard all year may perform badly because of illness, anxiety or a family problem on the day. In many countries, high-stakes exams cause enormous stress, and some students suffer from sleeplessness, depression and a fear of failure.

Continuous assessment offers a more complete picture of a student's ability. Because marks are spread across the year, students are encouraged to **work consistently** rather than only before exams. Projects and presentations can measure skills that exams ignore, such as research, creativity, teamwork and communication. Students also receive regular feedback, so they can improve before it is too late. On the other hand, continuous assessment can be time-consuming for teachers, and it is easier for students to copy work, use the internet dishonestly or receive too much help from parents.

Homework is closely related to this debate. Supporters argue that homework helps children **practise what they have learned** in class, develop self-discipline and form good study habits. It also allows parents to see what their children are studying. Critics, however, believe that too much homework leaves children tired and stressed and takes away time for play, sport, family and sleep, all of which are important for healthy development. Young children in particular may gain little from long homework tasks.

A balanced approach seems the most sensible. Schools can combine a smaller number of final exams with continuous assessment of projects and practical work, so that both knowledge and skills are measured. Homework should be **short, purposeful and suitable for the child's age**, rather than a daily burden.

In conclusion, no single method of assessment is perfect. Exams are fair and efficient but stressful and narrow, while continuous assessment is broader but harder to control. A combination of methods, together with a sensible amount of homework, is most likely to measure real learning and support students' well-being.

### The Role of Teachers and Parents

Children's education does not only take place at school. Parents and teachers both play a vital role in a child's academic and personal development, and IELTS questions often ask which of them has the greater influence or responsibility. The truth is that both are important, but in different ways and at different stages of a child's life.

Parents are a child's **first and most important teachers**. Long before children enter a classroom, they learn language, habits and values at home. Parents who talk to their children, read stories with them and encourage curiosity help them to develop a love of learning. In everyday life, it is easy to see that children whose parents show interest in their schoolwork, attend school meetings and provide a quiet place to study usually perform better. Parents also teach **moral values**, such as honesty, respect and hard work, which shape how children behave at school.

Teachers, on the other hand, have professional training and **subject knowledge** that most parents do not have. They know how to explain difficult concepts, how to organise learning step by step and how to assess progress. A good teacher can also inspire students to discover their talents and choose a career. For many children, especially those whose parents are busy, poorly educated or absent, a teacher may be the most positive adult role model in their lives.

Discipline is an area where both sides are often blamed. Teachers complain that some children arrive at school with no respect for rules, while parents complain that schools are too relaxed. In reality, discipline works best when home and school **send the same message**. If a school's rules are supported by parents at home, children quickly learn that bad behaviour has consequences.

There is also a growing concern about **parental pressure**. Some parents push their children very hard to achieve top grades, because they want them to enter a good university or a well-paid profession, or because they compare them with other children. Moderate expectations can motivate children, but excessive pressure can cause anxiety, loss of confidence and even a dislike of learning. Other children face the opposite problem and receive almost no encouragement because their parents are busy working or do not value education.

The ideal role of parents, therefore, is to **support rather than control**: to show interest, praise effort as well as results, help children to organise their time and stay in regular contact with teachers. The role of teachers is not only to pass on knowledge but also to **motivate and inspire** students and to notice when a child needs extra help.

In conclusion, parents and teachers should be seen as partners rather than competitors. Parents usually have the greatest influence in the early years and on values, while teachers have more influence on academic knowledge and skills. When the two cooperate closely, children have the best chance of success.

### Academic Subjects versus Practical Skills

A common debate in education is whether schools should focus on traditional academic subjects, such as mathematics, science, literature and history, or on practical skills that prepare young people for daily life and work. Many employers complain that graduates know a lot of theory but cannot solve real problems, communicate well or manage their own money. This has led to calls for schools to change what they teach.

Supporters of practical education argue that school should **prepare students for real life**. Skills such as managing a budget, cooking healthy food, basic first aid, using computers safely and understanding contracts are useful for everyone, yet they are rarely taught. Vocational subjects, such as electrical work, mechanics, design and agriculture, can lead directly to employment and are especially valuable for students who are not interested in university. In addition, many schools now teach **computer skills** alongside reading, writing and mathematics, because almost every job requires them.

Community work is another form of practical learning. In some schools, students must spend time helping elderly people, disabled people or local charities. Such experiences teach **empathy, responsibility and teamwork**, and help young people understand people from different backgrounds. Students also gain confidence and may discover a career interest.

However, academic subjects remain extremely important. Mathematics trains logical thinking, science teaches students to test ideas with evidence, and history and literature help them understand society, human behaviour and different points of view. Reading novels and poems may not seem directly "useful", but it improves language skills, imagination and the ability to **think critically**. These abilities are essential in many professions and in responsible citizenship.

There is also the risk of focusing too narrowly on job training. The job market is changing quickly, and specific practical skills may become out of date within a few years. A broad academic education gives students a foundation that allows them to **adapt to new situations** and learn new skills later in life. If children are pushed into a narrow vocational path too early, they may lose the chance to change direction.

The best solution is to combine both approaches. Academic subjects can be taught in a more practical way, for example by using real-life problems in mathematics lessons or by asking science students to design solutions for local issues. At the same time, schools can include short courses in financial literacy, digital skills and life skills, and give older students the choice of vocational or academic pathways.

In conclusion, schools should not have to choose between knowledge and skills. Young people need academic knowledge to think and understand, and practical skills to act effectively in the real world. A modern curriculum should **strike a balance** between the two.

### Technology in Schools: Computers or Teachers?

Technology has become a familiar part of school life. Many classrooms now have projectors, smart boards and computers, and students use tablets, educational apps and online platforms to complete their work. Schools, however, have limited budgets, so an important question arises: should they spend more money on technology, or on hiring and training good teachers?

Supporters of technology argue that it makes lessons more **interactive and engaging**. Videos, animations and simulations can bring difficult topics to life; for instance, students can explore the human body in three dimensions or watch a chemical reaction that would be too dangerous to perform in class. Computers also give students access to a vast library of information, and learning programs can adapt exercises to each student's level. Moreover, young people need strong **digital skills** for almost any future job, so using computers at school prepares them for the modern workplace.

Technology can also help teachers. Software can mark simple tests automatically, track each student's progress and identify those who need extra help. This saves teachers time that they can spend on planning lessons and supporting individual students. In schools with too few teachers, recorded lessons and online resources can partly **fill the gap**.

However, technology is expensive and can quickly become out of date. Computers need maintenance, software licences must be renewed, and devices may break or be stolen. Some schools have spent large sums on tablets that end up unused because teachers were never trained to use them in their lessons. In such cases, the money would have been better spent elsewhere.

More importantly, technology cannot replace the human qualities of a **good teacher**. A teacher can motivate a student who has lost confidence, notice when a child is unhappy at home, manage a noisy classroom and adapt a lesson when students do not understand. Teachers also act as role models, teaching values such as honesty, respect and perseverance. No computer program can offer the same encouragement, guidance and emotional support. In addition, too much screen time at school may increase distraction and reduce students' ability to concentrate on reading and writing by hand.

The quality of an education system depends above all on the quality of its teachers. Smaller class sizes, better salaries and ongoing training help schools to **attract and keep talented teachers**, which has a direct effect on students' learning. Technology is most effective when it is in the hands of a skilled teacher who knows when and how to use it.

In conclusion, schools need both technology and teachers, but they are not equally important. Investment in well-trained, motivated teachers should come first, because technology is only a tool. When schools combine good teaching with carefully chosen technology, students can **enjoy the benefits of both**.

### University Education and Employment

For many families, a university degree is seen as the key to a successful future. Parents make great sacrifices to send their children to university, believing that a degree will lead to a secure, well-paid job and a respected position in society. However, as more and more young people graduate, people increasingly ask whether a degree really guarantees a good job, and whether experience and soft skills are just as important.

There are good reasons to value university education. Many professions, such as medicine, law, engineering, teaching and architecture, **require a degree** by law or in practice. University also develops important abilities, including research, critical thinking, academic writing and independent study. On average, graduates tend to **earn higher salaries** and have more career choices than people without degrees. In addition, university introduces students to people from different backgrounds and helps them build professional networks.

However, a degree is no longer a guarantee of employment. In many countries, the number of graduates has grown faster than the number of graduate jobs. As a result, many young people with degrees are unemployed or work in jobs that **do not match their qualifications**. Employers frequently complain that graduates have theoretical knowledge but lack practical experience and soft skills, such as communication, teamwork, problem-solving and time management.

Experience can teach skills that a classroom cannot. A young person who works part-time in a shop learns how to deal with customers, handle money and solve problems quickly. Someone who starts as an apprentice electrician or builder gains **hands-on experience** and may become a skilled professional without ever going to university. Many successful entrepreneurs began their businesses with little formal education but a great deal of determination and practical knowledge.

It is also important to remember that society needs both kinds of people. Doctors and engineers are clearly valuable, but a city could not function without electricians, plumbers, mechanics, farmers and drivers. These workers often learn their skills through experience and training rather than degrees, and they deserve **respect and fair pay**. Treating university as the only route to success can make young people feel like failures if they choose a different path.

The most successful approach is to combine education with experience. Universities can include internships, practical projects and work placements in their courses, so that students graduate with both knowledge and real-world skills. Governments can also improve the status of **vocational training** so that it is seen as an equal alternative to university, not a second-best option.

In conclusion, a good university degree is valuable and opens many doors, but it does not guarantee a good job on its own. Employers look for people who combine knowledge with experience, adaptability and strong soft skills. Young people should therefore **gain practical experience** while studying and choose the path that best suits their abilities and goals.

### Studying Abroad, Exchange Visits and Foreign Languages

In a globalised world, more and more students study abroad, join international exchange programmes or learn foreign languages from an early age. Many schools now organise exchange visits in which teenagers spend a few weeks living with a host family and attending school in another country. These experiences can be life-changing, but they also involve costs and challenges.

The most obvious benefit of studying abroad is that it helps young people **broaden their horizons**. Living in another country exposes students to different customs, foods, values and ways of thinking. This can reduce stereotypes and develop **tolerance and cultural understanding**. A teenager who spends a month in Japan, for example, may return with a deeper appreciation of both Japanese culture and their own.

Exchange visits and study abroad also encourage independence. Away from their parents, students must manage their own money, solve everyday problems and make new friends in an unfamiliar environment. These experiences build **confidence and maturity**, which help them later at university and work. Students also improve their foreign language skills much faster when they are surrounded by native speakers every day.

Learning foreign languages, in general, brings many advantages. Speaking a second language, especially English, can **improve career prospects**, because many international companies and organisations require it. Language learning also improves memory and problem-solving, and it allows people to communicate directly with others when they travel. Many experts believe that children learn languages more easily when they start young, because they can copy pronunciation naturally and are less afraid of making mistakes. Others argue that primary school children should first master their own language, and that older children learn grammar more effectively.

However, studying abroad has disadvantages. It is often very expensive, so only wealthy families can afford it, which may increase inequality. Some students suffer from homesickness, loneliness or **culture shock**, and teenagers on short exchanges may feel anxious living with strangers. There are also safety concerns for parents, and the time spent abroad may interrupt students' studies at home.

At the national level, when talented students study abroad and decide not to return, their home country can lose skilled professionals. This problem, known as **brain drain**, is a serious concern for many developing countries, which spend money educating young people who later work elsewhere.

To maximise the benefits, schools and governments can offer scholarships for students from poorer families, prepare students carefully before exchange visits, and ensure that host families are properly selected. Countries can also encourage graduates to return by offering good career opportunities at home.

In conclusion, studying abroad, exchange visits and language learning offer valuable personal, academic and career benefits. Although they involve costs and risks, these can be reduced with good planning and support. On balance, the **advantages clearly outweigh the disadvantages** for most young people.

### Arts, Music, Sport and Physical Education

When schools are under pressure to improve exam results and prepare students for a technological economy, subjects such as art, music, drama and physical education are often the first to be reduced. Some people argue that these subjects are a luxury and that time and money should go to "useful" subjects like mathematics, science and information technology. Others believe that a good education must develop the whole child, not only their academic skills.

Those who want to focus on science argue that a country's development depends on **science and technology**. Engineers, doctors, programmers and researchers drive economic growth, solve medical problems and create new industries. If governments allocate more funding to science teaching, they believe, the country will become more competitive. With limited school hours, they ask why students should spend time painting or singing instead of learning skills that lead directly to jobs.

However, the arts bring benefits that are easy to overlook. Art, music and drama **encourage creativity and self-expression**, which are increasingly valued by employers in fields such as design, marketing, media and even engineering. Learning a musical instrument requires discipline, patience and concentration. Drama helps shy children to build confidence and speak in public. The arts also help young people to understand their own culture and history, and they can provide a way to express emotions and **reduce stress**.

Physical education is equally important. Many children today live a sedentary lifestyle, spending hours in front of screens and very little time moving. Regular PE lessons help to **tackle childhood obesity**, improve fitness and develop healthy habits for life. Team sports also teach important social skills, such as teamwork, leadership, fair play and how to win and lose with dignity. Many teachers also notice that pupils concentrate better in class after physical activity.

Some people argue that exercise is the responsibility of parents rather than schools. In reality, many parents work long hours and cannot take their children to sports clubs, and families in poorer areas may have no safe place to play. School may therefore be the only place where some children exercise regularly.

A balanced curriculum is the best answer. Science and technology deserve strong support, but they should not completely replace creative and physical subjects. Schools can integrate the arts with other subjects, for example through design projects in science, and ensure that every child has at least a few hours of physical activity each week. The most successful scientists and engineers often have creative interests too, which shows that the two areas **complement each other**.

In conclusion, arts and physical education are not a waste of time but an essential part of a **well-rounded education**. They develop creativity, health, confidence and social skills, which are just as important for a happy and successful life as academic knowledge.

### Discipline, Behaviour and School Life

Discipline is a growing concern in many schools. Teachers report that some pupils are disrespectful, use their phones in class or disrupt lessons for everyone else. At the same time, schools make many decisions about daily life, such as whether pupils must wear a uniform and whether boys and girls should be taught together or separately. These issues all affect the learning environment.

Good discipline is essential for learning. When a classroom is calm and orderly, teachers can teach effectively and students can concentrate. A single **disruptive pupil** can waste a large part of a lesson, which is unfair to the rest of the class. For this reason, some people believe teachers should be allowed to send disruptive children out of the classroom. This may protect other students' learning in the short term, but it does not solve the underlying problem, and the excluded child may fall further behind.

Bad behaviour usually has a cause. A child may be bored because the lessons are too easy or too difficult, may have learning difficulties that have not been identified, or may be facing problems at home. Effective schools therefore try to **address the root causes**. They talk to the child and the parents, provide extra support or counselling, and use positive methods, such as rewarding good behaviour, rather than relying only on punishment. Clear rules that are applied fairly and consistently are more effective than harsh punishments.

School uniforms are another common topic. Supporters argue that uniforms **create a sense of equality**, because children from rich and poor families look the same, which can reduce bullying about clothes. Uniforms also create a sense of belonging and prepare students for workplaces with dress codes. Opponents say that uniforms limit self-expression and can be expensive for poor families, and that a relaxed dress code allows children to feel comfortable and develop their own identity.

Another debate concerns **single-sex schools**. Some people believe that boys and girls learn better separately, because they may be less distracted and more confident to participate, and girls may be more likely to choose subjects like physics and engineering. Others argue that mixed schools reflect real society, where men and women live and work together, and that they help students learn to **respect the opposite sex** and communicate naturally.

Overall, a positive school culture depends on cooperation between teachers, parents and students. Parents should support the school's rules at home, and schools should involve students in creating rules so that they feel responsible for them. Activities such as sports teams, clubs and community projects also give pupils a sense of belonging, which naturally reduces bad behaviour.

In conclusion, discipline is necessary for effective learning, but it works best when it is fair, consistent and focused on helping children improve rather than simply punishing them. Decisions about uniforms and school types should aim to create a **safe, respectful and supportive environment** in which every child can learn.

### Lifelong Learning and Adult Education

Education was once seen as something that ended when a person left school or university. Today, because technology and the job market are changing so quickly, many people need to continue learning throughout their lives. This idea is known as **lifelong learning**. It includes adults retraining for new jobs, workers taking evening courses and elderly people learning new skills after retirement.

The most practical reason for lifelong learning is employment. Skills that were valuable twenty years ago may now be out of date, while new jobs require knowledge of computers, data and new technologies. Workers who **update their skills** regularly are more likely to keep their jobs, gain promotion or move into new careers. For employers, a workforce that keeps learning is more productive and adaptable. Online courses and part-time programmes have made it easier for adults to study while working and caring for their families.

Lifelong learning is also valuable for older people. Some believe that once a person is over 65, it is too late to learn. However, many elderly people learn to use smartphones, take up painting, study languages or attend local classes. Learning keeps the mind active, which may help to **slow down memory loss**, and it gives older people a sense of purpose after retirement. Classes are also an opportunity to make friends, which can reduce **loneliness and isolation**, a common problem among the elderly.

In addition, learning new technology helps elderly people stay connected and independent. An older person who can use video calls can see their grandchildren regularly, and one who can bank or shop online can manage daily tasks without always asking for help. In a world where many services are moving online, digital skills are becoming essential for everyone.

However, lifelong learning faces several barriers. Many adults have little free time because of work and family responsibilities. Courses can be expensive, and some people lack confidence because they had negative experiences at school or feel they are **too old to learn**. Elderly people may also have health problems, such as poor eyesight or hearing, which make learning more difficult.

Governments, employers and communities can help to overcome these barriers. Governments can provide free or low-cost courses, especially in digital skills, and employers can offer training during working hours. Libraries and community centres can run classes designed for older learners, with patient teachers and a relaxed pace. Families, too, can encourage older relatives to try new activities.

In conclusion, learning should not stop at the school gate. In a fast-changing world, lifelong learning helps people to **remain employable**, active and connected at every stage of life. It is never too late to learn, and societies that support adult education are likely to be healthier, happier and more productive.

| brain drain (n) | মেধা পাচার | **suffer from** brain drain | Developing countries suffer from brain drain. |
| lifelong learning (n) | আজীবন শিক্ষা | **promote / encourage** lifelong learning | Governments should promote lifelong learning. |
| update their skills | দক্ষতা হালনাগাদ করা | workers **need to** update their skills | Workers need to update their skills regularly. |

## Part 5 — Sentence Structure Toolkit {: .part}

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Paragraph mix formula:** প্রতিটি body paragraph-এ রাখার চেষ্টা করো → **1টি long simple + 1–2টি compound + 2–3টি complex + 1টি compound-complex** sentence। 5–6টি বাক্য × গড়ে 20 শব্দ = **100–120 words**।

</div>

#### 1. Long simple sentence (rich noun phrase)

**Formula:** [long noun phrase] + verb + object
*একটাই clause, কিন্তু subject-টা বড় ও তথ্যপূর্ণ।*

- The rising cost of university education in many countries **has prevented** talented young people from poor families from studying.
- A well-designed school curriculum with a balance of academic and practical subjects **prepares** students for adult life.

#### 2. Compound with *and / but / so / yet*

**Formula:** Clause 1, + and/but/so/yet + Clause 2

- Online courses are flexible, **but** they require a high level of self-discipline.
- Rural schools cannot attract good teachers, **so** their students often fall behind.

#### 3. Compound with semicolon + linker

**Formula:** Clause 1**; however / therefore / as a result,** Clause 2

- Exams are objective**; however,** they often test memory rather than understanding.
- Many graduates lack practical experience**; therefore,** universities should offer internships.

#### 4. Complex — concession (*Although / Even though / While*)

- **Although** free university education is expensive, it brings long-term benefits to the whole economy.
- **Even though** technology is useful in the classroom, it cannot replace a good teacher.

#### 5. Complex — reason (*Because / Since / As*)

- **Since** children spend most of their early years at home, parents have a strong influence on their values.
- **Because** employers value soft skills, students should take part in group projects and clubs.

#### 6. Complex — condition (*If / Unless / Provided that*)

- **If** governments do not invest in rural schools, the gap between city and village will continue to grow.
- Online learning can be effective, **provided that** students have a reliable internet connection.

#### 7. Relative clause (*which / who / where / whose*)

- Students **who** receive encouragement from their parents usually perform better at school.
- Many schools have reduced art and music lessons, **which** limits children's creativity.

#### 8. Compound-complex

**Formula:** Although/Because/If + clause, Clause A, and/but Clause B

- **Although** exams cause stress, they are fair to all students, **and** they are easy for universities to compare.
- **If** parents and teachers work together, children feel supported, **and** discipline problems become less common.

#### 9. Participle phrase

- **Living** with a host family abroad, teenagers quickly improve their language skills.
- **Faced** with high tuition fees, many students take part-time jobs while studying.

#### 10. *Not only … but also …*

- Physical education **not only** improves children's health **but also** teaches them teamwork.
- Studying abroad is **not only** an academic experience **but also** a lesson in independence.

#### 11. *The more …, the more …*

- **The more** parents pressure their children, **the more** anxious the children become.
- **The more** skills workers learn, **the easier** it is for them to find new jobs.

#### 12. Cleft sentence (emphasis)

- **It is** the quality of teachers, not the number of computers, **that** determines the quality of a school.
- **What** many students need most **is** encouragement rather than pressure.

#### 13. Impersonal passive

- **It is widely believed that** education is the key to national development.
- **It is often argued that** university degrees no longer guarantee good jobs.

#### 14. Contrasting views

- **While some people argue that** homework is essential, **others believe that** it causes unnecessary stress.
- **While** supporters of uniforms stress equality, **critics** focus on freedom of expression.

#### 15. Result / consequence

- Many talented teachers move to cities, **which means that** rural children are left with untrained teachers.
- Exams decide university places. **As a result,** students often memorise rather than understand.

#### Introduction (35–50 words)

প্রায়ই যুক্তি দেওয়া হয় যে আয় যা-ই হোক না কেন, শিক্ষার সুযোগ সবার জন্য খোলা থাকা উচিত, এবং প্রাথমিক বিদ্যালয় থেকে বিশ্ববিদ্যালয় পর্যন্ত শিক্ষার্থীদের কোনো খরচ দিতে হওয়া উচিত নয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমি এই মতের সাথে সম্পূর্ণ একমত, কারণ বিনামূল্যে শিক্ষা প্রতিটি শিশুকে ন্যায্য সুযোগ দেয় এবং পুরো সমাজকে উপকৃত করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — Idea 1 (100–120 words)

এই ধারণার সাথে আমার একমত হওয়ার প্রধান কারণ হলো, বিনামূল্যে শিক্ষা সব শিশুর জন্য সমান সুযোগ (equal opportunities) তৈরি করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> এর কারণ হলো, মেধা সমাজের প্রতিটি শ্রেণিতেই পাওয়া যায়, কিন্তু টাকা সবার কাছে থাকে না, তাই কে পড়তে পারবে তা কখনোই পরিবারের আয় দিয়ে ঠিক হওয়া উচিত নয়। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> বিশ্ববিদ্যালয়গুলো যখন উচ্চ টিউশন ফি নেয়, তখন নিম্ন আয়ের পরিবারের অনেক মেধাবী তরুণ কখনো আবেদনই করে না; ফলে তাদের যোগ্যতা নষ্ট হয় এবং তারা দরিদ্রই থেকে যায়। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, একজন দরিদ্র কৃষকের মেধাবী সন্তান যদি বিনা খরচে চিকিৎসাবিজ্ঞান পড়তে পারে, তাহলে সে ডাক্তার হতে পারে, এবং এই একজন স্নাতকই পুরো পরিবারকে আরও ভালো জীবনে তুলে আনতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> তাই প্রতিটি স্তরে বিনামূল্যে শিক্ষা অনেক পরিবারে দারিদ্র্যের চক্র (cycle of poverty) ভাঙতে সাহায্য করতে পারে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — Idea 2 (100–120 words)

আমার একমত হওয়ার আরেকটি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ কারণ হলো, বিনামূল্যে শিক্ষা অর্থনীতি ও সমাজ উভয়কেই উপকৃত করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> তরুণরা যখন কোনো ফি ছাড়াই পড়তে পারে, তখন দেশ একটি উচ্চশিক্ষিত কর্মীবাহিনী (workforce) পায়, যা দেশকে আরও উৎপাদনশীল, আরও উদ্ভাবনী এবং বিদেশি বিনিয়োগকারীদের কাছে আরও আকর্ষণীয় করে তোলে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যেমন, ঋণ ছাড়া পড়াশোনা শেষ করা একজন তরুণ প্রকৌশলী বাংলাদেশে থেকেই একটি ছোট কোম্পানি শুরু করতে পারেন, যা কর্মসংস্থান তৈরি করে এবং রাষ্ট্রের কর আয় বাড়ায়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে বিনামূল্যে উচ্চশিক্ষা অত্যন্ত ব্যয়বহুল, শিক্ষায় ব্যয় করা অর্থ আসলে খরচ নয়, বরং একটি দীর্ঘমেয়াদি বিনিয়োগ (long-term investment)। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে প্রতিটি স্তরে বিনামূল্যে শিক্ষা বেশি কর আদায় এবং সমাজকল্যাণ ও অপরাধ দমনে কম খরচের মাধ্যমে সরকারের টাকা ফেরত দেয়। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Conclusion (35–55 words)

#### Introduction (35–45 words)

কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে শিশুর পড়াশোনার অগ্রগতিতে শিক্ষকের প্রভাবই সবচেয়ে বেশি, অন্যদিকে অনেকে বিশ্বাস করেন যে বাবা-মায়ের প্রভাব আরও বেশি। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমি শেষের মতটিকে সম্পূর্ণ সমর্থন করি, কারণ বাবা-মা-ই শিশুর প্রথম ও সবচেয়ে গুরুত্বপূর্ণ শিক্ষক। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — বিপরীত মত (90–120 words)

একদিকে, যারা শিক্ষকের ভূমিকাকে সমর্থন করেন, তারা যুক্তি দেন যে শিক্ষকদের এমন পেশাগত প্রশিক্ষণ ও বিষয়ভিত্তিক জ্ঞান আছে, যা বেশিরভাগ বাবা-মায়ের নেই। <span class="tag">Complex</span> তারা জানেন কীভাবে কঠিন ধারণা ব্যাখ্যা করতে হয়, কীভাবে ধাপে ধাপে শেখানো সাজাতে হয় এবং কীভাবে অগ্রগতি মূল্যায়ন করতে হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> একজন দক্ষ শিক্ষকের মাধ্যমে কঠিন বিষয় সহজ করে তোলা যায়; ফলে শিক্ষার্থীরা নিয়মিত উন্নতি করে। <span class="tag">Compound · Passive</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, গণিতে দুর্বল একজন শিক্ষার্থী একজন ধৈর্যশীল শিক্ষকের সাহায্যে বিষয়টি উপভোগ করতে শিখতে পারে, এবং এতে তার ফলাফল ভালো হতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> এ কারণে অনেকে বিশ্বাস করেন যে পড়াশোনার অগ্রগতিতে শিক্ষকের প্রভাবই সবচেয়ে বেশি। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — আমার মত (90–120 words)

অন্যদিকে, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে বাবা-মায়ের প্রভাব বেশি, কারণ পড়ার অভ্যাস ও মনোভাব ঘর থেকেই শুরু হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> বাবা-মা যখন সন্তানের সাথে বই পড়েন এবং তার প্রশ্নের উত্তর দেন, তখন স্কুল শুরুর অনেক আগেই শিশুর মধ্যে জানার আগ্রহ তৈরি হয়, এবং শেখার এই ভালোবাসা বহু বছর থাকতে পারে। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, যে শিশুর বাবা-মা প্রতি রাতে গল্প পড়ে শোনান, সে বইয়ের প্রতি আগ্রহ নিয়েই স্কুলে আসতে পারে, যা তার পড়তে শেখা অনেক সহজ করে দেয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও কেউ কেউ যুক্তি দেন যে শিক্ষকদের বিষয়জ্ঞান বেশি, যে শিক্ষার্থীর পরিবার পড়াশোনাকে গুরুত্ব দেয় না (value education), তাকে সাহায্য করতে সবচেয়ে ভালো শিক্ষকও হিমশিম খান। <span class="tag">Complex</span> ফলে শিশুর পড়াশোনার অগ্রগতিতে বাবা-মায়ের প্রভাবই বেশি শক্তিশালী। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Conclusion (35–45 words)

পরিশেষে, শিশুর পড়াশোনার উন্নতিতে বাবা-মা নাকি শিক্ষকের প্রভাব বেশি, এ নিয়ে মানুষের মধ্যে ভিন্ন ভিন্ন মত আছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও শিক্ষকেরা মূল্যবান জ্ঞান ও দক্ষতা দেন, আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে বাবা-মা বেশি গুরুত্বপূর্ণ, কারণ পড়ার ভালো অভ্যাস ঘর থেকেই শুরু হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

### Essay 3 — Advantages Outweigh Disadvantages?

<div class="question">Some educationalists think that international exchange visits will benefit teenagers at school. To what extent do the advantages outweigh the disadvantages?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · EDU-01*

**Essay type:** Advantages–Disadvantages, Type 2 (outweigh) · **Position:** The advantages clearly outweigh the disadvantages.<br>
**অবস্থান:** সুবিধাগুলো অসুবিধার চেয়ে স্পষ্টভাবেই বেশি। তাই Although ও While বাক্যে আগে অসুবিধা, comma-র পরে সুবিধা।<br>
**কাঠামো:** IELTS_Advantages_Disadvantages_Essay_Guide.md অনুযায়ী — Type 2 (মতামতসহ) — Although-introduction ও While-conclusion-এ যে দিক সমর্থন করো তা comma-র পরে, প্রতিটি body paragraph-এ ঠিক 7টি বাক্য। মোট 330–360 শব্দ।

**পরিকল্পনা (Plan)**

- **Introduction (40–50):** বিনিময় সফর কী → Although + অসুবিধা (খরচ, মানসিক চাপ), + সুবিধা (দৃষ্টিভঙ্গি, আত্মনির্ভরতা) → In my opinion, সুবিধা বেশি।
- **Body Paragraph 1 (120–135) — সুবিধা, 7টি বাক্য:** দৃষ্টিভঙ্গি প্রসার → উদাহরণ: জাপানে এক মাস → আত্মনির্ভরতা ও ভাষা → আত্মবিশ্বাস ও পরিপক্বতা → সারাংশ।
- **Body Paragraph 2 (120–135) — অসুবিধা, 7টি বাক্য:** খরচ → উদাহরণ: অল্প বেতনের পরিবারের মেধাবী শিক্ষার্থী → homesickness ও culture shock → শেষ বাক্য: Although these… বৃত্তি ও প্রস্তুতি দিয়ে কমানো যায়।
- **Conclusion (35–45):** নতুন paraphrase → While + অসুবিধা, + সুবিধা → Therefore, I firmly believe…

#### Introduction (40–50 words)

এখন অনেক স্কুল শিক্ষা বিনিময় সফরের (exchange visits) আয়োজন করে, যেখানে কিশোর-কিশোরীরা কয়েক সপ্তাহ বিদেশে একটি আয়োজক পরিবারের সাথে থেকে পড়াশোনা করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও এ ধরনের সফর কিছু শিক্ষার্থীর জন্য ব্যয়বহুল ও মানসিক চাপের হতে পারে, এগুলো কিশোরদের দৃষ্টিভঙ্গি প্রসারিত করে এবং আত্মনির্ভরতা গড়ে তোলে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আমার মতে, এর সুবিধাগুলো অসুবিধার চেয়ে স্পষ্টভাবেই বেশি। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 1 — সুবিধা (120–135 words)

এতে কোনো সন্দেহ নেই যে আন্তর্জাতিক বিনিময় সফর কিশোরদের জন্য উল্লেখযোগ্য সুবিধা বয়ে আনে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> প্রথমত, সবচেয়ে বড় সুবিধা হলো, এমন একটি সফর তরুণদের দৃষ্টিভঙ্গি প্রসারিত করতে (broaden their horizons) সাহায্য করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> এর কারণ হলো, অন্য দেশে থাকার ফলে শিক্ষার্থীরা ভিন্ন রীতিনীতি, খাবার, মূল্যবোধ ও চিন্তাধারার সাথে পরিচিত হয়, যা সহনশীলতা গড়ে তোলে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> উদাহরণস্বরূপ, জাপানে এক মাস কাটানো একজন বাংলাদেশি কিশোর সময়ানুবর্তিতার মূল্য শেখে, এবং এই শিক্ষা প্রায়ই সারাজীবন থেকে যায়। <span class="tag">Compound-Complex</span> এছাড়া, একটি বিনিময় সফর শুধু আত্মনির্ভরতাই গড়ে তোলে না, শিক্ষার্থীদের বিদেশি ভাষার দক্ষতাও উন্নত করে। <span class="tag">Simple</span> একা একা সমস্যা সমাধান করার ফলে এই শিক্ষার্থীদের মধ্যে আত্মবিশ্বাস ও পরিপক্বতা (confidence and maturity) তৈরি হয়, যা পরে বিশ্ববিদ্যালয়ে ও কর্মজীবনে তাদের সাহায্য করে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> সব মিলিয়ে, বিদেশে অল্প কিছুদিনের অবস্থান কিশোরদের প্রাপ্তবয়স্ক জীবন এবং পরস্পর-সংযুক্ত, বহুসংস্কৃতির বিশ্ব — দুটোর জন্যই প্রস্তুত করে। <span class="tag">Simple</span>

#### Body Paragraph 2 — অসুবিধা (120–135 words)

অন্যদিকে, এটি অস্বীকার করা যায় না যে বিনিময় সফরের কিছু অসুবিধাও আছে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> প্রথমত, অনেক পরিবারের কাছে এই সফর ব্যয়বহুল মনে হয়, কারণ বিমান ভাড়া, বিমা ও দৈনন্দিন খরচ দ্রুত বেড়ে যায়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> আর্থিক সহায়তা ছাড়া নিম্ন আয়ের পরিবারের শিক্ষার্থীরা প্রায়ই এতে অংশ নিতে পারে না; ফলে সুবিধাগুলো মূলত ধনী শিক্ষার্থীরাই পায়। <span class="tag">Compound</span> যেমন, যে মেধাবী শিক্ষার্থীর বাবা-মা অল্প বেতন পান, তাকে হয়তো বাড়িতেই থেকে যেতে হয়, অথচ ধনী সহপাঠীরা বিদেশে যায়, যা অন্যায্য মনে হয়। <span class="tag">Complex</span> দ্বিতীয়ত, বাড়ির জন্য মন খারাপ (homesickness) ও সাংস্কৃতিক ধাক্কা একটি বড় বাধা হতে পারে, কারণ স্বল্পমেয়াদি সফরে কিশোররা অপরিচিত মানুষের সাথে থাকতে গিয়ে উদ্বিগ্ন বোধ করতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> তারা যদি দ্রুত মানিয়ে নিতে না পারে, তাহলে অভিজ্ঞতাটি আর উপভোগ করতে পারে না, এবং ফলস্বরূপ এ থেকে খুব কমই শেখে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> যদিও এই আর্থিক ও মানসিক চ্যালেঞ্জগুলো বাস্তব, স্কুলগুলো বৃত্তি (scholarships) ও যত্নশীল প্রস্তুতির মাধ্যমে এগুলো কমাতে পারে। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

#### Conclusion (35–45 words)

পরিশেষে, বিদেশে আয়োজক পরিবারের সাথে থেকে পড়াশোনা করা এখন স্কুলজীবনের একটি জনপ্রিয় অংশ হয়ে উঠেছে। <span class="tag">Simple</span> যদিও (While) এ ধরনের সফর ব্যয়বহুল ও মানসিকভাবে কঠিন হতে পারে, এগুলো কিশোরদের আরও উদার, আত্মনির্ভর ও আত্মবিশ্বাসী করে তোলে। <span class="tag">Complex</span> তাই আমি দৃঢ়ভাবে বিশ্বাস করি যে সুবিধাগুলো অসুবিধার চেয়ে অনেক বেশি। <span class="tag">Complex</span>

</div>

## Part 8 — Self-Check Checklist {: .part}

Use this after **every** translated essay (Part 6) and **every** homework essay (Part 9). Give yourself 1 point for each "Yes".

| # | Check | Yes / No |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did I answer **every part** of the question? | |
| 2 | Is my **position clear** in the introduction and the same in the conclusion? | |
| 3 | Does each body paragraph have **one main idea + explanation + example**? | |
| 4 | Did I use at least **5 complex** and **2 compound-complex** sentences? | |
| 5 | Did I use **8+ words or collocations** from the Word Bank / Part 4 correctly? | |
| 6 | Did I check **articles** (a/an/the), **plurals** and **subject–verb agreement**? | |
| 7 | Did I avoid repeating the same word more than **3 times** (e.g. "education", "students")? | |
| 8 | Did I follow my **essay-type guide** (Part 2G): its structure, its word plan (about **280 words**) and no partial opinion, in 40 minutes? | |

<div class="tip" markdown="1">

**Common mistakes in education essays (এগুলো এড়িয়ে চলো):**

- ❌ *Education are important.* → ✅ Education **is** important. (uncountable)
- ❌ *knowledges, homeworks, advices* → ✅ knowledge, homework, advice (uncountable)
- ❌ *The students should go to the university.* (general meaning) → ✅ Students should go to university.
- ❌ *Teachers teach to students.* → ✅ Teachers **teach** students.
- ❌ *learn* vs *study*: ✅ Children **learn** a language; students **study** for exams.
- ❌ *In my country, Bangladesh, education is very important in our life.* (too general) → ✅ *In recent years, the cost of higher education has become a major concern for many families.*
- **"students / children" প্রতিশব্দ:** pupils, learners, young people, school-age children, undergraduates, the younger generation

</div>

## Part 9 — Homework Essays {: .part}

Write these essays **alone in English**, without looking at Part 6. Plan for 5 minutes, write for 35 minutes, and check with the Part 8 checklist. There is **no model answer** — send your essay to the AI if you want feedback.

### Homework 1 — Agree / Disagree

<div class="question">The current trend in education is to move away from traditional exams and instead have continuous assessment over the school year. What do you think of this trend?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · EDU-12 · ★ exams vs continuous assessment*

**Essay type:** Agree / Disagree — one clear side · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 270–345 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 35–50 · BP1 Idea 1 100–120 · BP2 Idea 2 100–120 · Conclusion 35–55<br>
**Use ideas from:** Exams, Assessment & Homework; Teachers & Parents

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- প্রশ্ন "What do you think of this trend?" একটি প্রবণতার ওপর মত চায়, তাই Agree/Disagree guide: প্রবণতাটি ইতিবাচক নাকি নেতিবাচক — একটিই পক্ষ নাও। "দুটোর মিশ্রণই সবচেয়ে ভালো" লিখবে না।
- Idea 1 ও Idea 2 কী হবে (প্রকৃত বোঝাপড়া যাচাই, পরীক্ষার চাপ কমা)? প্রতিটির একটি বাস্তব উদাহরণ কী?
- বিপরীত যুক্তিটি (নকল সহজ, শিক্ষকের কাজের চাপ) BP2-এর Response বাক্যে রাখো: Although some people argue that…, [তোমার পক্ষ]।
- উপসংহার: In conclusion, I firmly believe that… + দুটি idea নতুন শব্দে। নতুন কোনো সুপারিশ বা idea নয়।

</div>

**Try to use:** continuous assessment · test memory rather than understanding · exam pressure · work consistently · receive regular feedback · fair and objective · copy work · strike a balance

**Sentence target:** use *While some people argue that…, others believe that…* once and *The more…, the more…* once.

</div>

### Homework 2 — Double Question

<div class="question">Some children receive almost no encouragement from their parents regarding their performance at school, while other children receive too much pressure from their over-enthusiastic parents, which can have a negative impact on the child. Why do you think some parents put too much pressure on their children to perform well at school? What do you think the role of a parent should be in their child's education?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · EDU-06*

**Essay type:** Double Question — List + List · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 = Q1 (List block) 90–120 · BP2 = Q2 (List block) 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** Teachers & Parents; University Education & Employment; Exams

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- দুটি প্রশ্নই কিছুর নাম জানতে চায় ("Why…?" ও "What… the role…?"), তাই দুটিই List block। "do you think" দেখে মতামত দেবে না; essay-র কোথাও "I" নয়।
- BP1: বাবা-মা কেন চাপ দেন — 2টি কারণ (প্রতিযোগিতামূলক চাকরির বাজার, অন্যের সাথে তুলনা) এবং একটি বাস্তব উদাহরণ।
- BP2: বাবা-মায়ের ভূমিকা — 2টি ভূমিকা (নিয়ন্ত্রণ নয়, সহায়তা; শিক্ষকের সাথে যোগাযোগ)। উদাহরণটি যেন ঠিক আগের Explain বাক্যকেই দেখায়।
- Introduction: paraphrase + [উত্তর 1], and [উত্তর 2]। Conclusion-এ দুটি উত্তর নতুন শব্দে, নতুন idea নয়।

</div>

**Try to use:** excessive parental pressure · a competitive job market · compare their children with others · lose confidence · support rather than control · praise effort · stay in contact with teachers · parental involvement

**Sentence target:** use one cleft sentence (*What children need most is…*) and one relative clause with *whose*.

</div>

### Homework 3 — Solutions Only

<div class="question">The gap between education in richer countries and education in poorer countries is a growing concern. What solutions can you suggest to deal with this situation?</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · EDU-15*

**Essay type:** Solutions only (Cause/Problem + Solution guide, Section 14) · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–300 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 30–40 · BP1 solution 1 90–120 · BP2 solution 2 90–120 · Conclusion 30–40<br>
**Use ideas from:** Inequality; Technology in Schools; Cost & Free Education

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- প্রশ্নে শুধু "solutions" চাওয়া হয়েছে, তাই প্রতিটি body paragraph-এ একটি সমাধান (N E E X L)। কোনো মতামত বা "This essay will…" নয়।
- সমাধান 1: দরিদ্র দেশের সরকার কী করবে (শিক্ষায় অগ্রাধিকার, শিক্ষকদের প্রণোদনা)? সমাধান 2: ধনী দেশ ও আন্তর্জাতিক সংস্থা কী করবে (অর্থ, প্রশিক্ষণ, অনলাইন পাঠ)?
- প্রতিটি সমাধানে WHO + WHAT + HOW আছে কি? প্রতিটির একটি বাস্তব উদাহরণ কী?
- Introduction: "[Who] can tackle this by [S1], and [who] can help by [S2]." উপসংহারে কোনো সমাধানকে "সবচেয়ে কার্যকর" বলবে না; দুটোই নতুন শব্দে।

</div>

**Try to use:** lack basic resources · a shortage of qualified teachers · offer incentives to teachers · international aid · online lessons · narrow the gap · give education a higher priority · a long-term investment

**Sentence target:** use *If…, …will…* at least twice and *not only… but also…* once.

</div>

### Homework 4 — Discussion

<div class="question">Some people think that schools should invest more money in technology, such as more computers, while others think more money should be spent on teachers. Discuss both views and give your opinion.</div>

*Source: IELTS Liz · EDU-19*

**Essay type:** Discussion (both views + opinion) · **Time:** 40 minutes · **Length:** 260–299 words<br>
**Word plan:** Intro 35–45 · BP1 (the other view) 90–120 · BP2 (your view) 90–120 · Conclusion 35–45<br>
**Use ideas from:** Technology in Schools; Online vs Classroom Learning; Teachers & Parents

<div class="bn" markdown="1">

#### ভাবনার প্রশ্ন (Thinking prompts)

- প্রযুক্তিতে বিনিয়োগের পক্ষে 2টি যুক্তি কী (আকর্ষণীয় পাঠ, ডিজিটাল দক্ষতা)?
- শিক্ষকে বিনিয়োগের পক্ষে 2টি যুক্তি কী (অনুপ্রেরণা, শৃঙ্খলা, প্রযুক্তি চালাতেও শিক্ষক লাগে)?
- তোমার নিজের মত কোনটি? সেটি BP2-তে রাখো এবং একটি বাস্তব উদাহরণ দাও (যেমন: ট্যাবলেট কেনা হলো কিন্তু প্রশিক্ষিত শিক্ষক নেই)।
- উপসংহারে নতুন কোনো idea বা সুপারিশ নয়: প্রশ্ন নতুন শব্দে, দুই মতের মূল কথা, আর একই মত।

</div>

**Try to use:** interactive and engaging lessons · digital skills · quickly become out of date · cannot replace a good teacher · attract and keep talented teachers · smaller class sizes · act as role models · invest in

**Sentence target:** use *It is… that…* once and a participle phrase (*Equipped with…, / Trained properly, …*) once.

</div>
